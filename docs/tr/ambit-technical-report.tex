\begin{abstract}
Embedding-based systems --- retrieval, retrieval-augmented generation,
deduplication, clustering --- depend on a single geometric property: items
that must be distinguished must remain distinguishable under the similarity
metric. We call the failure of this property \emph{crowding}. It is local:
a handful of documents can collapse together while every corpus-wide
average still looks normal. This report describes \textsf{ambit}, an
instrument that measures how a corpus occupies its embedding space. It
treats an embedded corpus as a pattern of points on a sphere and asks what
other sciences ask of point patterns: are some points packed more tightly
than chance would produce, how tightly, and which ones?

Three commitments follow. \textsf{ambit} measures occupancy at every
similarity level rather than at one chosen cutoff, because the choice of
cutoff decides the answer. It compares each measurement with a simulated
corpus of the same size and dimension whose points are spread as evenly
as chance allows. No real corpus is that even; the simulation shows how
far each corpus departs from chance. And it names individual documents,
because the useful output is a list of what to fix.

Each measurement translates into a statement about retrieval. A query
never lands exactly on its target, so when two documents sit close
together a query aimed at one can return the other --- a
\emph{collision}. From the distances between documents, \textsf{ambit}
computes how many collisions to expect at any level of query imprecision.
The largest imprecision a corpus absorbs before collisions begin is its
\emph{resolution bandwidth} $\sstar$.

Validation establishes three properties. On hundreds of corpora built
with no crowding, false alarms stay within the five percent that the
test allows. When near-duplicate groups are inserted into a corpus,
\textsf{ambit} recovers them in full; mean cosine, isotropy, and hubness
miss them. And across five public collections under seven encoders, with
every score locked before the answer keys were opened, the per-document
score identified the documents that retrieval would later fail on, in
twenty-six of twenty-nine testable pairings. The three exceptions are multi-hop questions, where failure comes from
needing a second document rather than from crowding.

In the case study --- one million documents from a public legal corpus ---
the measurements choose repairs and then verify them. Merging the
near-duplicate documents \textsf{ambit} names, at the similarity it
identifies as true duplication, improved twelve queries and harmed none
($p = 0.0005$); the same merge at a looser similarity degraded retrieval.
Penalty terms built from the measurements, added to a supervised
fine-tune, halve the damage it does outside its training data at no cost
to its gains. A rejection rule fixed in advance vetoed a defective
training round while the round's own loss was still falling. The
measurements govern a fine-tuning run well; used as the training
objective itself they leave retrieval unchanged. \textsf{ambit} is
unsupervised: no labels enter any measurement.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =========================================================================

An embedding model maps items to vectors so that semantic relatedness becomes
geometric proximity. Once a corpus is embedded --- typically onto the unit
sphere $\sphere$, with similarity the cosine of the angle between items ---
every downstream capability reduces to one primitive: \emph{given a direction,
list the items near it}. The primitive degrades in a specific way when
distinct items land too close together: queries cannot separate them, and the
system exhibits impostors at the top of rankings, near-duplicates that cannot
be ordered, and clusters that blur into one another. We refer to this failure
mode as \emph{crowding} and to its complement --- the corpus's ability to
keep distinct items distinct --- as \emph{resolution}.

An embedded corpus is
a \emph{pattern of points on a sphere}, and crowding is a question about
the arrangement of those points: do some of them bunch together more
tightly than chance would produce, how tightly, and which points are
they? Other sciences ask the same questions of their own point patterns:
ecologists of trees in a forest plot, epidemiologists of disease cases,
astronomers of galaxies. Answering them took those fields half a century
of statistical work. The methods they produced share three properties
this report needs: they read a pattern at every distance rather than at
one, they judge it against a stated model of no clustering, and they use
no bins. Embedding geometry is the same measurement problem on a new
domain. \textsf{ambit} applies those methods to the high-dimensional sphere and translates their output into the language of vector search (\S\ref{sec:related} credits each method's source).

Seen this way, crowding is difficult to measure for two reasons, which
\S\ref{sec:geometry} and \S\ref{sec:binned} develop. It is \emph{local}:
a collapsing pocket barely moves any global average. And the standard
instruments for localizing density --- histograms, hexbin lattices,
voxel grids, fixed-$k$ neighbor counts --- carry hidden discretization
parameters whose values change the answer. Figure~\ref{fig:introwhy}
previews both difficulties.

Three commitments shape its design. \textsf{ambit} measures occupancy
at every similarity level rather than at one chosen cutoff, because the choice of cutoff decides the answer (\S\ref{sec:binned}). It reads each measurement against a model of what the corpus would look like with no crowding --- a \emph{null model} --- so that the mild concentration that every learned encoder produces is not mistaken for a defect (\S\ref{sec:geometry}). And
it names individual documents rather than regions.
\S\ref{sec:geometry}--\S\ref{sec:power} build and validate the
instrument under these commitments; \S\ref{sec:training} and
\S\ref{sec:external} use its measurements to route deduplication and to
guard a supervised fine-tune.
Four contributions follow, each stated as what \textsf{ambit} adds
beyond the prior work it builds on (\S\ref{sec:related}).

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A calibrated instrument for occupancy and crowding}
  (\S\ref{sec:continuous}, \S\ref{sec:instrument}). The point-pattern
  toolkit of the observational sciences, composed into a single
  instrument for embedding spaces and released as an open library. It
  returns four readouts:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item the \emph{crowding curve} --- for every similarity level, the
    fraction of document pairs at least that similar;
  \item an \emph{occupancy discrepancy} --- one number for how unevenly the corpus fills the space. It condenses the whole curve, so a corpus can be tracked across encoder changes, repairs, or drift without comparing curves by eye;
  \item a \emph{per-document crowding radius} --- how far a document
    must reach to find company, which provably moves only slightly when
    the corpus changes slightly;
  \item a \emph{catalogue of tight groups}, found without choosing any
    similarity cutoff.
  \end{itemize}
  Each is read against a simulated corpus of the same size with no crowding. One such reference reproduces the narrow angular region into which learned encoders pack every document, so that this benign effect is not counted as a defect.
\item \textbf{Retrieval semantics for occupancy measurements}
  (\S\ref{sec:semantics}). An exact conversion from the distance between
  two documents to the chance that an imprecise query aimed at one
  returns the other. Summed over a corpus it gives the resolution
  bandwidth $\sstar$: how imprecise a query may be before the corpus
  starts returning the wrong document. The same conversion gives the temperature parameter of the widely used uniformity loss \cite{wang2020} a concrete meaning. This bridge from occupancy
  statistics to retrieval error counts is, to our knowledge, new.
\item \textbf{Blind evidence that geometry predicts retrieval failure}
  (\S\ref{sec:external}). We measured five public collections under
  seven public encoders and locked every readout before opening the
  answer keys. The per-document score then identified the documents that retrieval would later fail on, in $26$ of the $29$ pairings that test it (sign test $p \approx 8 \times 10^{-6}$). All three exceptions are multi-hop questions, where failure comes from
needing a second document rather than from crowding. The corpus-level
  $\sstar$ ranks encoders only where the task is similarity matching.
  Both boundaries come from measurements rather than assumptions.
\item \textbf{Measurement as a governor for fine-tuning}
  (\S\ref{sec:training}). The readouts choose a repair and then check
  it, without using labels for either. Merging the near-duplicate
  documents they name, at the similarity they identify as true
  duplication, improved twelve queries and harmed none ($p = 0.0005$);
  the same merge at a looser similarity made retrieval worse. Adding
  penalty terms built from the measurements to a supervised fine-tune
  halves the damage that fine-tune does outside its training data at no
  cost to its gains, and those terms provably act only on pairs close
  enough to be confused. A rejection rule fixed in advance vetoed a
  training round whose own loss curve looked healthy. The boundary is
  reported as plainly as the successes: used as the training objective
  itself rather than as a governor, the same measurements leave
  retrieval unchanged (\S\ref{sec:driving}).
\end{enumerate}

Throughout, \textsf{ambit} is \emph{unsupervised by design}: no labels, gold
pairs, or relevance judgments are consumed. The mathematics assembled here is, with small exceptions, classical, drawn from spatial statistics, directional statistics, geometric inference, and discrepancy theory (\S\ref{sec:related}). The contribution is the \emph{instrument}: the selection, calibration, and combination of these known statistics into a measurement system with stated guarantees, stated nulls, and stated scope, together with the negative results that ruled out the alternatives.

\section{Geometry of Crowding}
\label{sec:geometry}
% =========================================================================

Three elementary observations about high-dimensional spheres determine
the measurement design.

\subsection{Items are directions; a retrieval neighborhood is a patch of
the sphere}

Embeddings compared by cosine live on the unit sphere: each item is a
\emph{direction}, and similarity is the angle between directions. A
retrieval neighborhood --- ``everything scoring above $0.8$ against this
query'' --- is the set of directions within a fixed angle of the query,
which is a patch of the sphere's surface bounded by a circle (a
\emph{spherical cap}; Figure~\ref{fig:cap}). Every occupancy question then becomes a statement about caps. A corpus is concentrated when some caps hold more items than their share of the sphere's area predicts. An item is crowded when even a small cap around it already contains other items. And two items are confusable when they share one small cap: any query that lands near one cannot avoid the other, so they are \emph{future collisions}.

\subsection{In high dimension, ``random'' means ``orthogonal''}

Fix one item on $\sphere$ and picture the set of directions at least
$0.3$-similar to it: a cap around it (Figure~\ref{fig:cap}). In low dimension that cap is a sizeable patch of the sphere. As dimension grows, there are ever more ways for two directions to differ, so the sphere's area migrates toward the thin band $90^\circ$ from \emph{any} fixed direction. The cap's share of the surface collapses: at $d = 768$, the cap at $\cos \ge 0.3$ holds roughly $10^{-16}$ of the sphere's area. A uniformly placed second item essentially never lands in
it. Numerically, the cosine between two independent uniform directions
follows a bell curve centered on $0$ whose width shrinks as $1/\sqrt d$
($\mathcal N(0, 1/d)$). At $d = 768$ that width is
$1/\sqrt{768} = 0.0361$, matching the $0.0361$ measured on $4{,}000$
sampled uniform points (Figure~\ref{fig:conc}). Three consequences follow.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{``Similar'' is a geometrically tiny target.} Two unrelated
  items land in each other's $\cos = 0.3$ caps about once in $10^{16}$
  draws.
\item \textbf{The well-spread null has no small-distance tail at all.}
  In each of $19$ simulated uniform corpora of this size, the fraction
  of pairs with $\cos \ge 0.30$ is exactly zero, so \emph{any} observed
  mass there is unambiguous structure. (Nineteen is the classical
  envelope count: real data exceeding all $19$ simulations is a
  $1/20 = 5\%$ event under the null.) Distance concentration --- the
  classical curse of high dimension \cite{beyer1999} --- is therefore an
  asset here: every close pair is a finding.
\item \textbf{Averages over pairs are dominated by the background,}
  since almost every pair is a near-orthogonal background pair. The next
  subsection makes this precise.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Why averages cannot see crowding}
\label{sec:averages}

Every corpus-wide average of a pairwise quantity lives on the
\emph{square of all pairs}: an $n \times n$ grid with one cell per
ordered pair of items (Figure~\ref{fig:hide}). A group of crowded items
occupies a stretch of the square's \emph{side}; its pairs occupy the
small square block where that stretch meets itself. Side and area scale
differently: membership grows with the side, while the group's
\emph{pair share} --- the fraction of all pairs that fall inside it ---
grows with the area. A pocket holding $5\%$ of the items therefore owns
only $0.25\%$ of the pairs any average is taken over. The benchmark corpus used throughout this
report makes the dilution concrete. It holds $n = 4{,}000$ items at
$d = 768$, and the last $k = 200$ of them --- five percent --- are
replaced with near-duplicates of one another, so alike (median pairwise
cosine $0.929$) that a retrieval system cannot rank them apart.
\S\ref{sec:method} gives the full construction. Now follow the arithmetic of the
average, step by step. Five percent of the items yields
$0.05^2 = 0.25\%$ of the pairs, so $99.75\%$ of the average consists of
background pairs, which the previous subsection showed sit near
$\cos = 0$. Each pocket pair contributes a cosine of roughly $0.93$ at
that $0.25\%$ pair share, so the entire catastrophe adds
$0.0025 \times 0.93 \approx 0.002$ to the mean. Two hundred documents
became interchangeable, and the corpus-wide average moved from about
$0.000$ to $0.002$: invisible without a calibrated test, and
uninformative about \emph{which} items failed. Covariance-spectrum summaries (effective rank, isotropy scores) fail for
the same arithmetic reason: they are sums over the corpus, and sums are
dominated by the diffuse majority. Global averages address whether the
whole cloud is degenerate, while crowding concerns which part of the
cloud has collapsed, how tightly --- at $\cos = 0.9$, say, rather than
at $\cos = 0.5$ --- and which items it contains. A sound instrument must therefore do four things, and
\S\ref{sec:continuous} builds each of them.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Read whole distributions rather than averages.}
\item \textbf{Read them at every similarity level,} since pockets live
  at the tight levels the bulk never reaches.
\item \textbf{Calibrate against a matched null,} since an absolute
  cosine value carries no meaning on its own.
\item \textbf{Name the individual items,} since the actionable output is
  a list of ids.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% =========================================================================

The introduction framed an embedded corpus as a point pattern and
crowding as the clustering question the observational sciences ask of
point patterns. Those sciences have mature statistics for answering it, and
\textsf{ambit} applies them to the high-dimensional sphere where
embedded corpora live. Four fields contribute:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{spatial statistics} --- the crowding curve and the test
  that decides whether it is significant;
\item \textbf{geometric inference} --- the per-document crowding radius
  and the catalogue of tight groups;
\item \textbf{discrepancy theory} --- the occupancy discrepancy;
\item \textbf{directional statistics} --- the reference that reproduces
  the cone.
\end{itemize}
Each paragraph below first describes a method as its home field uses it, then states what changes on embeddings. None of these fields supplies the retrieval layer: none has a notion of one document outranking another at a query. The
conversion of pair distances into expected retrieval collisions
(\S\ref{sec:semantics}) is therefore \textsf{ambit}'s own, and it is
what turns these borrowed statistics into a retrieval instrument.

\paragraph{Counting pairs at every distance.}
Ripley's $K$ function \cite{ripley1976,besag1977} is the standard test
for whether a set of points clusters. An ecologist applies it to trees
in a forest plot, an epidemiologist to the home addresses of disease
cases, an astronomer to galaxies in a survey volume, a cell biologist to
proteins imaged on a membrane. The recipe is the same in each: count how
many pairs fall within every distance, and compare those counts to what
scattering the same number of points at random would give. The comparison must be read at every distance, because a pattern can be clumped at one distance and evenly spread at another: trees cluster into groves while the groves sit far apart. Any single radius reports one of these and hides the other.

\textsf{ambit}'s crowding curve is this idea applied to the sphere, and
two things change in the move. First, the random-scatter comparison
stays exact rather than becoming an approximation: on a sphere the
chance that a random point falls within a given distance of another is
just the area of the surrounding patch of surface, which has a closed
form (the cap-area law). Second, a correction the planar version needs
becomes unnecessary. A forest plot has edges, and a tree near one has
neighbours only on the inside, so its pair counts run low and the
ecologist must compensate for it (an \emph{edge correction}). A sphere
has no edge --- walk in any direction and the surface continues --- so
every document sits in the same position and nothing needs correcting
\cite{robeson2014}. Neither property is a contribution of this report;
both are consequences of the domain, and they are noted because they are
what makes the transfer clean.

\paragraph{Testing a whole curve at once.}
Reading the counts at every distance creates a problem of its own. The
curve is not one number but a value at each of some eighty similarity
levels, and each level carries its own range of counts that
crowding-free corpora produce --- a band the observed curve should stay
inside. Checking each level separately against its own band does not work: every check carries some chance of a false alarm, and across eighty checks one almost always occurs. On this report's own crowding-free corpora, that level-by-level reading raised an alarm on all forty (\S\ref{sec:power}).

The repair is to treat the curve as a single object. A global envelope
test \cite{myllymaki2017} builds $199$ crowding-free corpora, computes
each one's whole curve, and asks where the observed curve ranks among
all two hundred by its most extreme departure at any level. That yields
one verdict covering every level at once, with a smallest attainable
$p$ of $1/200 = 0.005$, since the observed curve can at best exceed all
$199$ simulations. This is \textsf{ambit}'s liftoff test. It also sets a division of labor that the report uses throughout. The band shows at which similarity level a corpus
departs from the reference --- at $\cos = 0.82$ on the legal corpus,
say, rather than anywhere looser. The single global verdict decides
whether that departure is real.

\paragraph{Conditioning on an uneven background.}
Spatial statisticians met \textsf{ambit}'s central confound long ago.
Disease cases cluster in cities, but that says nothing about contagion,
because people cluster in cities. Counting pairs against a uniform
comparison would measure where the population lives rather than how the
disease spreads. The \emph{inhomogeneous} $K$ function
\cite{baddeley2000} repairs this by comparing the pattern against a
background that already contains the uneven population, so that only
clustering beyond it is reported.

The same repair applies on the sphere. A learned encoder does not spread
documents over the whole sphere; it packs them into a narrow region of
it, called a cone. That cone is \textsf{ambit}'s uneven background. The
reference of \S\ref{sec:kcurve} models it with a distribution from
directional statistics, the angular central Gaussian, and
\textsf{ambit} then reports only the clumping that the cone does not
explain.

\paragraph{What not to transfer.}
The same sciences documented the hazards of partitioned statistics. The
modifiable areal unit problem (MAUP)
\cite{gehlke1934,openshaw1979,openshaw1984} and the ecological fallacy
\cite{robinson1950} are the classical demonstrations that
partition-based statistics change with the partition and say nothing
about individuals --- the two failures, quantified on embedding displays
in \S\ref{sec:binned}, that \textsf{ambit} is built to avoid. The same
lesson appears in one dimension as the histogram's bin-width and origin
sensitivity \cite{scott1979,freedman1981,silverman1986}; the averaged
shifted histogram \cite{scott1985} repairs placement by averaging over
origins, and \textsf{ambit} removes the partition altogether.

\paragraph{Auditing every region at once.}
A \emph{region} here is a patch of the sphere: every direction lying
within some angle of a chosen centre. There are infinitely many such
patches, at every centre and every size, so asking whether each holds
its fair share of documents is an audit that cannot be run directly.
Stolarsky's invariance principle \cite{stolarsky1973,brauchart2011}
converts it into one computable number, the mean pairwise distance.
\textsf{ambit}'s occupancy discrepancy is this theorem put to work.

\paragraph{Scoring one point, and finding groups.}
Two questions remain after the curve: how crowded is \emph{this}
document, and which documents form a tight group.

For the first, the \emph{distance to a measure}
\cite{chazal2011,chazalmichel2021} asks how far a point must reach to
gather a fixed share of the data --- a small radius means crowded, a
large one isolated. It replaces density estimation, which fails in high
dimension, and it is stable: changing the corpus a little provably
changes every score only a little. \textsf{ambit}'s per-document
crowding radius is this estimator on the sphere.

For the second, a \emph{cluster tree} \cite{hartigan1981,chaudhuri2010}
records which groups exist at which density, so groups can be read at
all densities at once instead of at one chosen threshold. ToMATo
\cite{chazal2013} and HDBSCAN's condensed tree \cite{campello2015} are
two standard forms of that object \cite{rolle2020}, and both inherit
stability from persistence theory \cite{cohensteiner2007}.
\textsf{ambit}'s catalogue of tight groups is this machinery with one
addition: a group is reported only if it is tighter than the tightest
group pure chance produces at the same corpus size.

Gao et al.\ \cite{gao2019} and Ethayarajh \cite{ethayarajh2019}
established the cone drawn above, and measured its consequence: packing every vector into a narrow band raises all pairwise cosines at
once. A corpus can then look crowded everywhere while nothing is harder
to tell apart. Judging such a corpus against an even spread would report
the cone; judging it against a reference that already contains the cone
reports only what the cone does not explain --- the third panel. For this reason every readout is calibrated against a reference that contains the cone (an \emph{anisotropy-matched} reference) rather than against the uniform sphere alone. Mu et al.\ \cite{mu2018} showed that subtracting the mean direction and a few dominant components restores much of the usable angular range. That is the linear repair that \textsf{ambit} recommends when its measurements attribute crowding to the cone alone. Cai et al.\
\cite{cai2021} found genuine cluster structure \emph{inside} the cone,
which is what the anisotropy-matched reference is designed to isolate.

\paragraph{What the embedding literature measures instead.}
Effective rank \cite{roy2007} and IsoScore \cite{rudman2022} compress
the covariance into one number --- roughly, how many directions carry
variance, and how round the cloud is. \textsf{ambit} reports both as
context, and \S\ref{sec:power} measures that they cannot see small
pockets. Hubness \cite{radovanovic2010} --- a few items appearing in disproportionately many nearest-neighbor lists --- is a symptom of the same congestion that \textsf{ambit} measures geometrically, and it serves as a baseline in the detection-power study. Wang and Isola
\cite{wang2020} decompose contrastive embedding quality into
\emph{alignment} (paired texts land together) and \emph{uniformity}
(everything spreads out); \textsf{ambit} is unsupervised, so it measures only the uniformity half; \S\ref{sec:external} shows the same split in the measured results.

\paragraph{Embedding-quality scores.}
RankMe \cite{garrido2023}, $\alpha$-ReQ \cite{agrawal2022}, and their
relatives (surveyed in \cite{tsitsulin2023}) score an embedding, without
labels, by how evenly its variance spreads across directions. On unit vectors these all read the same underlying object, the covariance spectrum, so they mostly restate one another; the Vendi score
\cite{friedman2023,pasarkar2024} escapes the family by counting
effective modes through a nonlinear kernel
\cite{giraldo2015,ospanov2024}. \textsf{ambit} takes the lesson as a
design filter (\S\ref{sec:filters}): rather than add another reading of
the spectrum, it measures different objects --- the pair-distance
distribution and per-item fields.

\paragraph{Projections and density.}
Two-dimensional layouts preserve neighborhoods but not density
\cite{wattenberg2016,narayan2021}: a visually dense region of a
projection need not be dense in the embedding, which is why
\textsf{ambit} draws projections but never measures on them. Kernel density estimation converges at $O(n^{-4/(4+d)})$, a rate that becomes meaningless when $d$ is in the hundreds \cite{silverman1986}; the per-item layer therefore rests on the distance to a measure instead. Local intrinsic dimension estimators \cite{levina2005,facco2017} are used only per item, and only to compare items with one another.

\section{Experimental Methodology}
\label{sec:method}
% =========================================================================

Every claim marked \emph{measured} in this report follows the protocol
stated here.

\paragraph{Datasets.}
Claims are tested against synthetic data, where the answer is known by
construction, and against public corpora, where the geometry is real.
The public side splits into two roles --- one corpus studied in depth,
and a grid of collections scored against relevance judgments --- so
three bodies of data appear below.

\emph{(i) The synthetic benchmark family --- known ground truth by
construction.} Detection, localization, and recovery claims can only be
scored against a defect whose membership and scales are known exactly.
The way to obtain one is to build the corpus and insert the defect
deliberately, choosing which items belong to it and how tightly they
sit --- a \emph{planted} pathology. Ground truth then comes from the
construction, and the instrument still never consumes a label. One
instance of this family is followed end to end through the report; we
call it the \emph{traced} benchmark. It holds $n = 4{,}000$ points at
$d = 768$ (a contemporary encoder width), drawn as normalized standard
Gaussians, which places them exactly uniformly on $\sphere$. The
planted group --- a \emph{pocket} --- replaces the last $k = 200$
points ($5\%$) with
$x_i = (c + s\, g_i)/\lVert c + s\, g_i\rVert$ for one shared uniform
direction $c$, i.i.d.\ Gaussian $g_i$, and spread $s = 0.01$. That spread makes the pocket a group of near-duplicates: the median
cosine inside it is $0.929$, so a retrieval system cannot rank its
members apart. Yet every global average stays essentially unmoved
(\S\ref{sec:averages}). A defect this severe and this invisible is a
stringent test for a local diagnostic. The uniform control is the same draw
without the replacement. The wider family varies what the traced instance fixes: pocket count
($1$--$20$), size ($20$--$800$), and tightness ($s = 0.005$--$0.06$,
spanning near-duplicate to loose association). It adds mean-shift cones,
low-rank collapses, and pure nulls. Together these form the grid behind
the sensitivity, calibration, and power studies. All
generators are stated in full above or beside their experiments; nothing is
tuned to the instrument.

\emph{(ii) A public real corpus --- realistic geometry at scale.} Synthetic corpora cannot show the learned-embedding pathologies that
motivate the instrument. The case study (\S\ref{sec:instrument})
therefore uses \emph{Nemotron-Pretraining-Legal-v1} \cite{nemotronlegal},
a public (CC-BY-4.0) legal corpus published by NVIDIA. From four of its
thirteen subsets --- the California Code of Regulations, case-law
summaries, CaseHOLD, and eCFR question--answer pairs --- we take $10^6$
documents. We embed them at $d = 1024$ with the Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B
instruction-tuned encoder \cite{qwen3embedding}, L2-normalize them, and
store them as sharded Parquet with stable document ids. It supplies what the benchmark cannot: a real anisotropy cone (mean pair
cosine $+0.236$), and \emph{diffuse} within-subset crowding rather than
planted pockets. It also brings enough documents to exercise the streaming path, and
subset labels, which are used only to \emph{condition} one readout and
never enter a measurement.

\emph{(iii) A public evaluation grid --- external outcomes.} The external study (\S\ref{sec:external}) and the retrieval-facing
experiments (\S\ref{sec:routing}--\S\ref{sec:guardrail}) use five public
retrieval collections with relevance judgments and seven public
encoders, listed with citations in Tables~\ref{tab:corpora}
and~\ref{tab:encoders}. Judgments are quarantined: every readout is
computed and hash-frozen before any judgment file is opened, and judgments
are consumed exclusively by scoring code. The three do different jobs: the benchmark makes claims falsifiable,
the corpus makes them representative, and the grid scores them against
outside judgments.

\paragraph{Standard settings.}
These defaults govern statistics defined in
\S\ref{sec:continuous}--\S\ref{sec:semantics}, and each statistic is
introduced fully where it is defined. Unless an experiment sweeps them:
\begin{itemize}
\item pair statistics use $2 \times 10^5$ sampled distinct pairs;
\item whole-curve inference uses the global rank-envelope test at $199$
  null curves ($99$ inside report figures);
\item the anisotropy-conditioned reference is sampled from the corpus's
  own covariance eigenvalues at matched pair count;
\item the per-item crowding field (the distance to a measure, DTM) uses
  mass fraction $m = 0.02$;
\item the merge tree uses minimum pocket size $8$;
\item the discrepancy $z$ uses $64$ null replicates.
\end{itemize}
Each default is itself validated by a sweep reported in the section that
uses it.

\paragraph{Per-experiment protocols.}
Each experiment below states the hypothesis under test, the design and
the reason it takes that shape, the control it is read against, and the
measure that decides. A schematic of the design accompanies each; the
schematics carry no data. Full parameter values appear beside each
result.

\paragraph{Lattice fragility (Table~\ref{tab:maup}).}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Hypothesis:} the headline
statistics of binned displays are properties of the lattice rather than of the data.
  \item \textbf{Design:} hold $4{,}000$ synthetic 2-D points fixed and perturb only the
lattice, shifting its origin by fractions of one cell and changing its
column count. Any change in the display can then only come from the
lattice.
  \item \textbf{Control:} an isotropic jitter of the \emph{data} by $0.1\%$ of
their span, which shows how far the display moves when the data
themselves change by a comparable amount.
  \item \textbf{Measure:} the change in the display's peak
cell count and peak location under each perturbation, lattice against
control.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Confusion-law verification (Table~\ref{tab:flip}).}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Hypothesis:} the
closed-form probability that noise flips a ranking is exact in any
dimension.
  \item \textbf{Design:} two fixed unit vectors at a known angle, with $3 \times 10^5$
independent noise draws per cell of a $6 \times 4$ grid of angles and
noise scales. This simulates the law's own setting directly, so there
is no modeling assumption an error could hide behind.
  \item \textbf{Control:} the closed form itself; under
the hypothesis the residual is pure Monte-Carlo noise of predictable
size.
  \item \textbf{Measure:} the maximum absolute deviation between empirical flip rate
and formula over the grid.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Redundancy of the confusion and uniformity functionals (Table~\ref{tab:redundancy}).}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Hypothesis:} the two functionals may order
corpora identically, making one redundant.
  \item \textbf{Design:} eight synthetic corpus
generators spanning the pathology families, both functionals computed on
each --- redundancy is a claim about \emph{orderings across corpora}, so
the design produces orderings rather than values.
  \item \textbf{Control:} perfect rank
agreement, the outcome under full redundancy.
  \item \textbf{Measure:} the rank correlation, and each individual
disagreement, which is the more informative of the two.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Calibration and detection power (\S\ref{sec:power}).}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Hypothesis:} the instrument's alarms fire at their nominal rates on healthy corpora
and detect planted pathologies that standard summaries miss.
  \item \textbf{Design:} first, $400$ corpora with no defect (\emph{pure nulls}), used to
measure false-alarm rates and to set every detector's threshold at the
same $1\%$ specificity, since a power comparison is fair only when
every detector is equally reluctant to fire; then a grid of planted
pathologies, $50$ replicates per cell, with threshold seeds disjoint
from test seeds.
  \item \textbf{Control:} the pure nulls, and
the standard summaries (mean cosine, isotropy scores, effective rank,
hubness, a neighbor-distance quantile) run under identical thresholds.
  \item \textbf{Measure:} empirical false-alarm rate against nominal; detection rate per
pathology cell at matched specificity.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Sensitivity sweeps.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Hypothesis:} every default parameter sits on a
plateau, so reasonable variation does not change conclusions.
  \item \textbf{Design:} sweep each knob (reservoir size, pair-sample size, mass fraction, minimum
pocket size, null-replicate count) one at a time over an order of
magnitude or more.
  \item \textbf{Control:} the readout at the default, with seed-to-seed
spread as the scale of comparison.
  \item \textbf{Measure:} readout drift across the sweep,
compared to that spread.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The measurements as the training objective
(Table~\ref{tab:training}).}
This is the first of two ways the measurements can enter training. Here
they are the \emph{objective itself}: the thing being minimized, with no
other loss present. The guardrail experiment below is the second, where
they are instead a \emph{supplement} to someone else's objective.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Hypothesis:} penalties built from the measurements
reduce retrieval collisions without destroying the embedding's
structure.
  \item \textbf{Design:} a planted pocket with a linear map as the
smallest possible trainee, and a $20\%$ slice held out from mining,
weighting, and batching --- verdicts must come from data the training
machinery never touched.
  \item \textbf{Control:} the corpus before training, and a
structure-preservation score against the frozen base.
  \item \textbf{Measure:} expected collisions among the \emph{flagged}
items --- the documents whose base-model collision counts mark them as
most crowded --- measured on the held-out slice, and neighbor overlap,
per schedule.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The encoder loop (\S\ref{sec:loop}).}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Hypothesis:} held-out geometric readouts alone, with no labels, can judge
fine-tuning rounds: accept the safe ones, reject the harmful ones.
  \item \textbf{Design:} a fixed
stratified $200{,}000$-document subset with $20\%$ held out, and a
continuation rule registered before any round ran, so acceptance cannot
be negotiated after seeing results.
  \item \textbf{Control:} the base model's readouts, and the trainee's own loss
curve --- the signal a practitioner would otherwise trust.
  \item \textbf{Measure:} per-round held-out $\sstar$, flagged-cohort
collisions, and neighbor overlap against the rule.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Routing (\S\ref{sec:routing}).}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Hypothesis:} a repair succeeds only
at the site and scale the measurements prescribe --- not because
deduplication is generically good.
  \item \textbf{Design:} run the same label-blind deduplication in all four combinations of two
sites (foreign distractors; the target corpus) and two scales (the
confusable window; the duplicate scale). Only the full $2 \times 2$
separates ``the prescription was right'' from ``any cleanup helps.''
  \item \textbf{Control:} the other three combinations, which the
measurements do not prescribe.
  \item \textbf{Measure:} frozen-query failures
fixed and introduced per cell, with an exact paired test.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The measurements as a supplement to a supervised objective
(\S\ref{sec:guardrail}).}
The second of the two ways the measurements can enter training: added to
a supervised recipe as extra terms, rather than replacing its objective.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Hypothesis:} extra penalty terms built from the
measurements --- \emph{guard terms} --- prevent the damage
supervised fine-tuning does outside its training distribution, without
reducing its gains.
  \item \textbf{Design:} one supervised
recipe run twice, the arms differing \emph{only} in the guard terms, so
any outcome difference is attributable to them; the held-out measurement
gate is read and recorded before any judgment.
  \item \textbf{Control:} the unguarded
arm.
  \item \textbf{Measure:} the retrieval metric the fine-tune targets, where the claim is no
cost; the open-corpus metrics it does not target, where the claim is
protection; and the gate's pre-label prediction.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The external validation suite (\S\ref{sec:external}).}
One protocol run over every corpus--encoder pair, so the entries below
describe thirty-five instances of the same experiment rather than a
single one.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Hypotheses:} first, that
each document's expected-collision count predicts which judged-relevant
documents fail at their own queries; second, that the corpus-level
$\sstar$ ranks encoders within a corpus.
  \item \textbf{Design:} every corpus--encoder cell is embedded and fully measured, and the

  \item \textbf{Control:} for the per-document claim, the natural geometric baseline

\end{itemize}

\section{The Fragility of Binned Occupancy}
\label{sec:binned}
% =========================================================================

Binned density views --- hexbin lattices over a 2-D projection, voxel
grids, radial shells, fixed-$k$ neighbor counts --- are the standard
tools for localizing density. \textsf{ambit} uses none of them, and this
section measures why. Each of these views works by dividing
the space into cells and counting how many items land in each. The
trouble is that the cells are a choice --- where their boundaries fall,
and how large they are --- and that choice changes the counts. Two
analysts with the same corpus and different grids can report different
crowded regions, and the gap between their answers is larger than the
defects worth detecting.

Figure~\ref{fig:maup} and Table~\ref{tab:maup} measure both choices on
the tool's own display \cite{openshaw1984}: where the cell boundaries
fall, and how large the cells are. Moving the lattice half a
cell moves the headline peak count from $310$ to $403$, and moves which
cell holds the peak (the \emph{zonation} effect). Changing the cell size
varies the peak count by a factor of $2.4$ across reasonable column
counts (the \emph{scale} effect). Neither perturbation touches a single
data point. In the classical extreme of the same phenomenon, re-drawing the zones
over fixed data can move a correlation almost anywhere in $[-1,+1]$
\cite{openshaw1979}.

Binning also wastes data. A histogram's accuracy improves more slowly with sample size than a
kernel estimator's: its average squared error across the whole density
(the \emph{mean integrated squared error}) shrinks as $O(n^{-2/3})$
against $O(n^{-4/5})$ \cite{scott1979,freedman1981}. The partition therefore costs precision as well as reliability. The
averaged shifted histogram \cite{scott1985} repairs part of the loss:
it averages over many lattice placements, which removes the placement
dependence and recovers the kernel rate. \textsf{ambit} retains one hexbin
\emph{display}, averaged over placements this way and captioned as a view rather than a measurement.

\subsection{Every bin commits to one scale}

Crowding appears at different similarity levels. Near-duplicate pockets
sit above $\cos \approx 0.9$, topical clusters nearer $\cos \approx 0.5$,
and the anisotropy cone spreads across the whole range. Every bin width,
shell count, and fixed neighbor count $k$ silently commits to one of
them.
Inject $200$ near-identical points --- the benchmark's planted-pocket size,
$5\%$ of its $n = 4{,}000$ --- inside an already-dense cell. The hexbin peak moves from $340$ to $540$ --- a rise that looks no
different from an ordinarily busy cell. On the same corpus the continuous per-item field
of \S\ref{sec:dtm} responds far more sharply: its first percentile falls
by a factor of $18$. A fixed $k$ in a kNN graph is the
same defect in rank-based form: in a dense pocket $k{=}10$ spans a tiny
radius, while in a sparse outer region it spans a huge one.
``Neighborhood'' therefore means a different similarity level at every
point, which is the drift a crowding measure must exclude.

\subsection{What a cell count cannot do}

Four further costs are structural.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Instability.} A bin count is a step function of the data:
  nothing bounds how far a small perturbation can move it. The field of
  \S\ref{sec:dtm} carries the opposite guarantee --- move the corpus a
  little and every score provably moves only a little (a Lipschitz bound
  in the input's Wasserstein distance) --- and it holds up in practice,
  with self-correlation $0.997$ under jitter at $d{=}768$.
\item \emph{No null.} A peak count carries no statement of what a
  well-spread corpus would show; a perfectly uniform cloud still has a
  hottest cell.
\item \emph{No names} (the ecological fallacy \cite{robinson1950}). A
  cell count cannot say which items are crowded, and the actionable
  output of a diagnosis is a list of ids.
\item \emph{Projection.} Binning a 2-D layout measures the projection,
  not the object: far-apart groups collide in a cell, orthogonal
  crowding vanishes, and nonlinear layouts actively destroy density
  \cite{wattenberg2016}.
\end{itemize}
The repair is not a better density estimate: kernel density estimation
needs far more points than any corpus can supply once $d$ reaches the
hundreds \cite{silverman1986}. The repair is to drop partitions altogether and work directly with the
distances between points, using functions that commit to no scale of
their own and whose guarantees do not weaken as the dimension grows.

\section{Continuous Occupancy}
\label{sec:continuous}
% =========================================================================

The measurement layer is four readouts, which together meet the four
requirements of \S\ref{sec:averages}: the crowding curve, the occupancy discrepancy, the
per-document crowding radius, and the catalogue of tight groups.
Let $x_1,\dots,x_n \in \sphere$ (unit-normalized; cosine
$c_{ij} = \langle x_i,x_j\rangle$; chord $r_{ij} = \lVert x_i - x_j\rVert =
\sqrt{2 - 2 c_{ij}}$). \textsf{ambit} computes exact full-corpus second
moments by streaming and draws a bounded uniform \emph{reservoir} (a
fixed-size random subsample kept during the single pass) plus a large
random-pair cosine sample for everything else. The pair-sample default
of $2{\times}10^5$ is sized so that the scale readouts of this section
are stable to a few thousandths; the measured concentration rates appear
with the discrepancy statistic below. The figures below show the real corpus (Nemotron-Legal,
\S\ref{sec:method}), so the primary examples come from learned-embedding
geometry rather than synthetic constructions. The traced benchmark of \S\ref{sec:averages}, whose planted pathology
is known exactly, gives every layer a companion test in which the right
answer is known.

\subsection{The crowding curve}
\label{sec:kcurve}

\begin{definition}[Pair-closeness curve]
$K(t) = \Pr_{(i,j)}[\, c_{ij} \ge t \,]$, hereafter the crowding curve: the fraction of distinct pairs at
least $t$-similar, estimated \emph{exactly} from the pair sample by sorting
--- no bins, no origin, no width.
\end{definition}

$K(t)$ is Ripley's pair-counting test (\S\ref{sec:related}) carried to the
sphere \cite{ripley1976} and written in cosine coordinates. At each
similarity level it reports the fraction of pairs at least that close,
read against what the same number of points scattered at random would
give. Two references at
matched sample size accompany it (Figure~\ref{fig:kcurve}):

\emph{(i) The uniform null, tested globally.} For i.i.d.\ uniform points
the pair cosine satisfies $c = 2B - 1$, $B \sim
\mathrm{Beta}\!\big(\tfrac{d-1}{2},\tfrac{d-1}{2}\big)$, so null pair samples are drawn directly, without constructing a single
$d$-dimensional vector. The sphere has no boundary, so the edge
corrections of \S\ref{sec:related} neither apply here nor can be
misapplied \cite{robeson2014}.

Inference over the whole curve uses a one-sided global
\emph{rank-envelope} test \cite{myllymaki2017}. The test works by
simulation: the data curve is ranked among $199$ curves drawn from the
null, so the smallest attainable $p$ is $1/200$, and a single global $p$
covers all scales at once. Many curves tie, because every null curve is zero over most of the
range. Ties are broken by how extreme each curve becomes at its most
extreme point (its \emph{extreme rank length}). The $\alpha$-critical
envelope is the band the null curves span at the test's significance
level, and \emph{liftoff} is the highest cosine at which the data escape
it. Liftoff is annotated only when the global test rejects. On $40$
pure-null corpora the gated test reported $0$ liftoffs. The naive
pointwise band (the max of the null curves) fired on \emph{every} null seed
at the bulk edge --- a direct demonstration of the multiple-comparison
failure --- and even the $\alpha$-critical envelope alone over-fires
$\approx 4\times$ nominal. The division of labor is therefore strict: the
envelope locates the scale, and the rank $p$ decides significance.

\emph{(ii) The anisotropy-conditioned null.} Real corpora are cone-shaped for benign reasons, so judging them against
an even spread alone reports a defect where there is none. The second reference is the \emph{angular central Gaussian} (ACG) of
directional statistics \cite{tyler1987}, built from the corpus's own
covariance spectrum: $x = \Sigma^{1/2} g / \lVert \Sigma^{1/2}
g\rVert$, which rotation invariance lets us sample from the eigenvalues
alone. It reproduces the corpus's cone without its clustering, so any
height of the data curve above it is crowding that the cone cannot
explain.

Figure~\ref{fig:kcurve} shows the curve on the real corpus
(Nemotron-Legal, \S\ref{sec:method}): the global test rejects at the minimum
attainable $p = 1/200$ with liftoff at $\cos \approx 0.81$. Through
mid-similarities
the data track the anisotropy-conditioned reference, so that part of the
excess over uniformity is the cone itself, benign and expected of a
learned encoder. Above $\cos \approx 0.8$ the data rise above both
references: genuine tight-pair structure at the near-duplicate scale,
which \S\ref{sec:dtm} attributes to named documents. On the traced benchmark the same test
rejects at $p = 1/200$ with liftoff $\cos \approx 0.93$ and a shoulder of
excess mass exactly matching the planted pocket's pair share
($2.4{\times}10^{-3}$) --- the counterpart with known ground truth.
The lower panel of Figure~\ref{fig:kcurve} repeats the reading across six
corpora under one encoder. Every corpus carries tight-pair mass far above
the null, but the anatomy differs: ESCI and TREC-COVID hold the most
near-duplicate mass, Quora the least beyond its paraphrase band. A single
scalar would average that structure away.

\subsection{The occupancy discrepancy (Stolarsky $z$)}

A binned count inspects one cell of one lattice. The complete version
of that question inspects every spherical cap --- every center, every
radius --- and asks how far each cap's occupancy departs from the
uniform expectation. A classical identity collapses this total departure
into a single pairwise average.

\begin{proposition}[Stolarsky \cite{stolarsky1973}]
For points on $\sphere$, the spherical-cap $L_2$ discrepancy --- the squared
deviation between empirical and expected cap occupancy, integrated over
\emph{all} cap centers and \emph{all} cap radii --- equals, up to explicit
constants, a constant minus the mean pairwise chord
$\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i,j}\lVert x_i - x_j\rVert$.
\end{proposition}

The mean pairwise chord therefore performs the audit no single lattice
can: it covers every patch of the sphere at every size, and there is no
lattice left to choose. \textsf{ambit} reports it as a $z$-score against
the uniform null at matched pair-sample size, so that sampling noise is
accounted for.
Measured on the benchmark over $20$ seeds, the uniform control scores
$z = -0.14 \pm 1.00$: a mean at zero and unit spread, which is what
calibration looks like. Planting the $5\%$ pocket moves the mean chord
from $1.41405$ to $1.41160$, a shift of $0.17\%$, which the concentrated
null resolves as $z = -47 \pm 4$. Tightening
the pocket further (members within a cap of angular radius $\approx 0.15$)
drives the same statistic to $z = -430$: detection strength scales with the
pathology while the control stays at noise level.

Two properties govern how $z$ is read.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{$z$ is a test statistic rather than an effect size.} Its magnitude
  grows with the pair-sample size $P$, because the null mean's spread
  shrinks as $1/\sqrt P$. Values are comparable only at matched $P$, and
  the instrument reports them at its standard $P = 2{\times}10^5$. The
  scale readouts $\sstar$ and liftoff behave like effect sizes instead,
  concentrating as $1/\sqrt P$: $\mathrm{sd}(\sstar)$ falls from $0.0126$
  to $0.0013$ as $P$ grows from $10^4$ to $10^6$.
\item \textbf{The null's own spread must be estimated well.} With $24$
  null replicates $|z|$ is inflated by $\approx 20\%$ and reproduces with
  $\mathrm{sd} \approx 8$. The default of $64$ replicates sits where that
  inflation has died away; there the benchmark reads $z = -43 \pm 4.5$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{definition}[Empirical DTM \cite{chazal2011,chazalmichel2021}]
For mass fraction $m$ and $k = \lceil m n\rceil$:
$\dtm_m(x_i) = \big(\tfrac1k \sum_{j=1}^{k} r_{i,(j)}^2\big)^{1/2}$, with $r_{i,(j)}$ the
distance to $x_i$'s $j$th nearest reservoir point --- the radius the item
needs to gather an $m$-share of the corpus.
\end{definition}

A small radius means the item is crowded; a large radius means it is
isolated (Figure~\ref{fig:dtm}). The single parameter $m$ is a \emph{mass fraction}: a share of the
corpus rather than a count, so it needs no adjustment as $n$ grows. It

Between the global curve and the per-item field sits \emph{structure}:
whether the corpus forms one broad pile or many tight pockets, and which
documents constitute each. \textsf{ambit} answers with a merge hierarchy, built by single linkage
on a modified distance: the pair distance, raised to at least each
endpoint's distance to its own $k$th neighbor, so that a lone point
cannot bridge two groups (the \emph{mutual-reachability} metric)
\cite{campello2015}. The
tree is condensed at a minimum pocket size, and pockets are selected by
excess of mass in $\lambda = 1/\mathrm{distance}$: on each branch, the
cluster persisting over the widest scale range is retained. This is
equivalently $H_0$
persistence with the elder rule (at each merge, the younger component
dies) \cite{chazal2013,rolle2020}. Each selected pocket reports birth, death, prominence (death minus
birth: the scale range over which it persists as a separate entity),
size, and member ids
(Figure~\ref{fig:tree}). Three implementation findings matter.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Elder-rule inversion.} The naive elder rule is wrong for
  this purpose: the tightest pocket is the \emph{eldest} component and
  survives to the root, so the bulk ``dies into it.'' Excess-of-mass
  selection resolves the inversion.
\item \textbf{Caterpillar membership.} Single-linkage chains inside a
  tight pocket are caterpillars (long chains with single-node side
  branches). Membership is therefore reported as the condensed core plus
  the side branches that fell out in the tight regime (below the
  geometric mean of birth and death), excluding bulk stragglers.
\item \textbf{The childless root.} Chaining can prevent \emph{any} real
  split from existing: a tight pocket may seed the one component that
  then absorbs the bulk point by point, so no node ever has two children
  of the minimum size, the only cluster is the root, and the tightest
  structure in the corpus goes unreported. A parameter sweep exposed this
  failure --- at minimum size $32$ a planted $200$-member pocket returned
  nothing, and $3$ of $20$ benchmark seeds missed it even at the default.
  The fix treats a childless root as a candidate with death at the final
  merge scale, whereupon the membership rule extracts exactly the pocket
  core.
\end{itemize} With the fix in place, the tree recovers the planted pocket
in $20$ of $20$ seeds at exact size ($200.0 \pm 0.0$; birth $0.063$, death
$0.898$, prominence $0.835$) across min-sizes $8$--$64$, and on a
two-pocket variant recovers sizes $60$ and $40$ exactly.

Reported prominence carries a \emph{null floor}. Uniform corpora of
matched size and dimension produce maximum prominences of only
$\approx 0.005$ (99th percentile $0.009$ over $400$ nulls), so the
instrument simulates that floor on every run and suppresses any pocket
within it. A genuine pocket sits two orders of magnitude above the floor.
Figure~\ref{fig:tree} shows the resulting readout on the real corpus: one
pocket survives the floor ($204$ members, sample ids on its bar), and one
further candidate was suppressed.

\subsection{What the measurements cost, and what was excluded}
\label{sec:filters}

All continuous-layer statistics run on the reservoir or the pair
sample. The curve and its nulls are $O(P \log P)$ in the pair-sample
size. The DTM field is a blocked Gram top-$k$, $O(n_r^2 d)$ on a capped
subsample (seconds on CPU). The merge tree is a dense Prim MST on
$n_r \le 4{,}096$ rows plus a linear condensation pass. Full-corpus second moments stream in one pass.

Candidate metrics were admitted by five filters. They are recorded
here because they make future proposals cheap to evaluate.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Spectrum:} does it reduce, under a linear kernel, to the
  covariance spectrum already reported?
\item \emph{Continuity:} is it a continuous functional of the data, or
  a step function of bin, rank, or partition membership?
\item \emph{Null:} does it come with a calibrated reference?
\item \emph{Naming:} can it score individual items, or only regions?
\item \emph{Supervision:} is it label-free?
\end{itemize}
These excluded, among others: RankMe, NESum, and stable rank (spectrum);
$k$-means-conditioned statistics (continuity); raw counts (null); all
cell aggregates (naming); and neural-collapse metrics \cite{papyan2020}
(supervision).

% =========================================================================
\section{Operational Semantics: the Resolution Bandwidth}
\label{sec:semantics}
% =========================================================================

Occupancy statistics say how evenly a corpus covers the sphere. A
practitioner needs something else: how imprecise a query may be before
the wrong document wins. An exact formula connects the two, and
simulation confirms it.

\subsection{An exact pairwise confusion law}

A query that intends document $x$ lands near it rather than on it. The
simplest model of that is $q = x + \sigma g$ with $g \sim \mathcal N(0,
I_d)$, where $\sigma$ sets how far the query strays. A competitor $y$
then wins --- a \emph{collision} --- whenever $\langle q, y\rangle >
\langle q, x\rangle$.

\begin{proposition}[Exact flip law]\label{prop:flip}
For unit vectors at chord distance $r = \lVert x - y \rVert$,
$\;\Pr[\langle q, y\rangle > \langle q, x\rangle] =
\Phi\!\big({-r}/{2\sigma}\big)$, exactly, in any dimension.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Geometrically (Figure~\ref{fig:bisector}): the set of queries for
which $y$ beats $x$ is the half-space beyond the perpendicular bisector
of $x$ and $y$. The query starts at $x$, at distance $r/2$ from that
boundary, and isotropic Gaussian noise carries it across with
probability $\Phi(-(r/2)/\sigma)$. Algebraically: $\langle q, y -
x\rangle$ is Gaussian with mean $\langle x, y - x\rangle = \cos\theta
- 1 = -r^2/2$ and standard deviation $\sigma \lVert y - x\rVert =
\sigma r$, so the flip probability is
$\Phi\big((-r^2/2)/(\sigma r)\big) = \Phi(-r/2\sigma)$.
\end{proof}

Simulation confirms the law across a grid of angles and noise scales
(Table~\ref{tab:flip}). One consequence matters most. When two documents
are nearly identical ($r \to 0$), a query aimed at either returns the
wrong one about half the time, at every noise scale, however small. No
amount of query precision rescues them. This is why duplicate pockets
are the worst crowding pathology, and why the crowding curve's liftoff
at the near-duplicate scale (\S\ref{sec:kcurve}) matters operationally.

\subsection{$C(\sigma)$, $\sstar$, and the uniformity bound}

Summing the law over competitors gives the expected number of items
outranking the intended target,
\[
C(\sigma) = \sum_{j \ne i} \Phi\!\left(-\frac{r_{ij}}{2\sigma}\right)
\;\approx\; (n{-}1)\!\int \Phi\!\left(-\frac{r}{2\sigma}\right) dK(r),
\]
a quantity that depends only on the distances between pairs of
documents. Adding up the individual probabilities can only overstate the
chance that at least one competitor wins, no matter how those events
correlate (the union bound). $C$ therefore errs on the high side.
Hubness --- a few items recurring as near neighbors of many others ---
correlates the events and makes the bound loose, never wrong.

\begin{definition}[Resolution bandwidth]
$\sstar$ is the largest $\sigma$ with $C(\sigma) \le 1$ --- the corpus's
tolerance for imprecise queries, the most noise a query can carry before
one wrong item is expected to outrank the right one. $C$ is monotone, so
bisection inverts it.
\end{definition}

Figure~\ref{fig:csigma} compares the real corpus with its uniform
control. Nemotron-Legal crosses the one-collision tolerance at
$\sstar = 0.123$, against $0.148$ for a well-spread corpus of the same
size and dimension: it retains $83\%$ of the ideal noise budget. On the
benchmark, the planted $5\%$ pocket spends $26\%$ of the budget
($\sstar\!: 0.203 \to 0.150$).

The scale $\sigma$ is noise per coordinate, but it converts into
familiar cosine units. A noisy query's expected similarity to its own
target decays as $\langle \bar q, x\rangle \approx (1 + \sigma^2
d)^{-1/2}$. At $d = 1024$, $\sstar = 0.123$ therefore means a query may
drift until its cosine with its target falls to $\approx 0.25$ and still
be expected to win.

The lower panel overlays six corpora under one encoder. Their
tolerance crossings span $\sstar = 0.090$ to $0.129$. This is the
comparison the instrument is built to support: one encoder, six corpora,
and one number each for how much query noise that corpus tolerates.

\begin{proposition}[Uniformity as a Chernoff bound]\label{prop:chernoff}
With $U_t = \log \E\, e^{-t\lVert x - y\rVert^2}$ the uniformity functional
of \cite{wang2020}: $\;C(\sigma) \le (n{-}1)\, e^{U_t}$ at
$t = 1/(8\sigma^2)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\Phi(-z) \le e^{-z^2/2}$ with $z = r/2\sigma$.
\end{proof}

The temperature parameter of the widely used uniformity loss is thus not
arbitrary: $t = 2$ is a collision bound at query noise $\sigma = 0.25$. \textsf{ambit} reports
uniformity with this reading attached.

\subsection{Scope}

The guarantee has three boundaries.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Query population.} The corpus is treated as its own query
  population. This is exact for deduplication, clustering, and
  corpus-as-queries retrieval; extrapolating to an external workload
  assumes the workload lands where the documents are.
\item \emph{Noise shape.} The model's noise is isotropic --- the same
  in every direction --- while real query drift has structure. To test
  how much this matters, we re-ranked a grid of corpus types under
  structured noise of the same overall size. The isotropic ranking
  survives moderately structured noise (rank-$25$ subspace noise: $0.99$
  pairwise-order agreement over five seeds). It degrades only when the
  noise model is badly wrong (rank-$2$: $0.79$; noise aligned with each
  corpus's own top principal directions: $0.82$). Comparative use is
  therefore robust; noise built to align with the corpus is the stated
  failure mode.
\item \emph{Aggregation.} The aggregate is an upper bound: $C$
  over-counts confusions, never under-counts them.
\end{itemize}
$\sstar$ is therefore a conservative guarantee about one corpus at a
time, and it is strongest used comparatively --- the same corpus
embedded two ways, or the same embedding tracked over time.

certificate would need a different metric carrying its own stability
theorem, which is an open question rather than an engineering task.

\section{Calibration and Detection Power}
\label{sec:power}
% =========================================================================

The instrument's statistics were calibrated against $400$ corpora built
with no crowding, so that any alarm they raise is a false alarm by
construction. Four hundred is the number needed to pin a $1\%$
false-alarm rate to a standard error near $0.5\%$. Detection power was
then compared against a suite of standard diagnostics. Every threshold, the instrument's and the baselines' alike, was set on
those same nulls at a matched $1\%$ false-alarm rate, so every detector
is equally reluctant to fire.

\paragraph{Null calibration.}
On crowding-free corpora ($n = 2{,}000$, $d = 256$; the benchmark
generators with no defect planted), every alarm fires close to the rate
it advertises.
\begin{itemize}
\item the whole-curve rank test rejects at $p \le 0.01$ in $1.25\%$ of
  them, against a nominal $1\%$;
\item liftoff, which is reported only when that test rejects, fires in
  $6\%$ at $\alpha = 0.05$, against a nominal $5\%$;
\item the occupancy discrepancy exceeds $|z| > 3$ in $0.75\%$;
\item the largest pocket prominence has median $0.005$ and 99th
  percentile $0.009$ --- the reporting floor of \S\ref{sec:tree}.
\end{itemize}
Measured false-alarm rates for all calibrated thresholds land on
$0.010$--$0.013$.

Table~\ref{tab:power} reports detection power on every planted defect in
the benchmark family, and three readings follow.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Full power on every pocket.} The continuous layer --- the
  rank test, $\sstar$, the DTM tail ratio, and pocket prominence ---
  detects every planted pocket, including the hardest cell: a single
  pocket of $20$ items holding $1\%$ of the corpus. On that cell mean
  cosine ($0.04$), hubness ($0.18$), and the kNN-distance quantile
  ($0.22$) sit near the false-alarm floor, because too little of the
  corpus participates to move any average. This is the claim of
  \S\ref{sec:averages} in numbers.
\item \textbf{The exceptions.} Covariance-spectrum statistics (IsoScore)
  also reach full power on a single coherent pocket. When $1\%$ of the
  corpus shares one direction, that direction alone carries enough
  variance to stand out from the rest, so a spectrum summary is enough to
  detect one tight pocket at the corpus level. Three things it still
  cannot do: name the pocket's members, detect a mean-shift cone (power
  $0.00$, since centering removes the shift), or state what the crowding
  costs in retrieval terms.
\item \textbf{The discriminating cells behave as predicted.} The
  occupancy discrepancy $z$ is weak on small pockets, because a small
  pocket owns a tiny pair share. The pocket detector declines to alarm on
  a cone ($0.12$), which is correct: a cone is not a pocket. And hubness
  is blind to a low-rank collapse.
\end{itemize}
The layer's value is therefore not one more powerful summary number.
It detects everything the baselines detect, at the same false-alarm
rate, and it adds three things they cannot supply: the names of the
documents involved, the similarity level at which the trouble sits, and
a count of the retrieval errors it implies.

\subsection{Architecture}

\textsf{ambit} is a library with a thin CLI, and one canonical in-memory
type (\texttt{EmbeddingSet}) flows through its pipeline. A
\emph{streaming scan} makes one pass over the input --- \texttt{.npy},
\texttt{.npz}, \texttt{.parquet}, \texttt{.csv}, or \texttt{.jsonl}, as
a single file or a sharded directory --- accumulating exact second
moments, norms, and a uniform reservoir. A context builder then derives projections (PCA by default),
the eigenspectrum, a kNN graph, unsupervised clusters, and the
pair-cosine sample. The graph can be filtered to \emph{reciprocal}
neighbors, which suppresses hubs by construction.

All measurements of \S\ref{sec:continuous}--\S\ref{sec:semantics} are
library functions over these objects, and a separate presentation layer
(Appendix~\ref{sec:report}) renders them. The core depends on
\textsf{numpy} alone; heavier capabilities (tabular IO, ANN graphs,
UMAP, GPU paths, on-the-fly embedding via an OpenAI-compatible endpoint,
the training extra) are optional and degrade gracefully.

A comparison mode aligns two embeddings of the same items by id. It
reports a local number --- how many of each item's nearest neighbors
survive the change --- beside global similarity statistics (centered
kernel alignment, CKA \cite{kornblith2019}, kernel and rigid distances).
The two can disagree, and the disagreement is informative: high CKA with
low neighbor overlap means the change kept the broad shape of the space
while rewriting who sits next to whom.

\texttt{.npy}; the Parquet path adds decode overhead, and the case

the entire continuous layer $\approx 1.8$\,s.

Dimension is the variable that does cost. It enters through the scan's
$d^2$ scatter accumulation and the context build's $d^3$
eigendecomposition, the one super-linear phase at $52.6$\,s for

The approximate mode carries one demonstrated hazard. With the planted
pocket placed in the corpus's \emph{last} rows --- the layout that
label-sorted shards produce --- the full scan detects it (rank test
$p = 0.010$, liftoff $0.92$). Approximate scans of the first
$5{\times}10^4$ to $3{\times}10^5$ rows see a clean corpus
($p = 0.18$--$0.45$, no liftoff, mean cosine $\approx 0$). An
approximate scan's reservoir is only as representative as the row order
that fed it. The mode therefore suits exploratory iteration. It is unsafe for
conclusions about data whose layout may correlate with its content.

$0.090$), yet it still uses most of its dimensions: effective rank $728$
of $1024$. Its occupancy discrepancy sits at $z = -4{,}211$, and
the whole-curve rank test rejects uniformity at the minimum attainable
$p = 0.005$ with liftoff at $\cos \approx 0.82$: genuine tight-pair
excess at the near-duplicate scale. The anisotropy-conditioned reference attributes most of that
discrepancy to the cone itself.

The whole-curve test earns its place here. Reading each similarity
level separately would put the liftoff at $\cos \approx +0.07$, a false
alarm at the edge of the bulk of the kind \S\ref{sec:power} quantifies.
Testing the curve as one object moves the significant excess up to the
near-duplicate levels, where it belongs.

Operationally the corpus keeps $83\%$ of the ideal noise budget: a
query may drift to cosine $\approx 0.25$ against its target before a
wrong document is expected to win. The per-item field's low tail is real
but shallow, and the merge tree finds one modest pocket. Crowding here
is therefore \emph{diffuse within subsets} rather than pocketed. In that
regime global averages mislead optimistically, while per-item counts
still name the at-risk documents. The separability panel (labels provided, used only to condition the
readout) reports two numbers that disagree. Neighborhood purity is
$0.95$: a document's nearest neighbors nearly always come from its own
subset. The silhouette is $+0.04$, near zero: the subsets occupy no
regions of their own. The corpus is locally coherent yet globally
entangled within the cone, and no single number could report both
facts.

\section{Governing Fine-Tuning with Measurements}
\label{sec:training}
% =========================================================================

The instrument finds crowding, locates it, and counts its cost in
expected retrieval errors. This section tests a stronger claim: that its
measurements can also \emph{drive} interventions. The confusion law is differentiable, so the measurements
can enter training in one of two roles --- as the training
\emph{objective itself}, or as a \emph{supplement} to an objective
someone else has already chosen. Four uses are tested:
\begin{enumerate}
\item the measurements as the objective itself, on a worked example
  (below);
\item that same objective run as a gated loop on the case-study encoder
  (\S\ref{sec:loop});
\item execution of the deduplication that the readouts prescribe
  (\S\ref{sec:routing});
\item the measurements as a supplement to a supervised objective, in the
  form of guardrails (\S\ref{sec:guardrail}).
\end{enumerate}
The two roles separate sharply, and the separation is the section's main
finding. Used as the objective itself, the measurements do not improve
retrieval: the tuned encoder is retrieval-neutral against its base, and a
label-supervised reference is far ahead of both. Used as a supplement ---
a \emph{governor} over an objective someone else chose --- they work
well. Extra penalty terms built from the measurements halve the damage
supervised fine-tuning does outside its training data at no cost to its
gains, a gate rejects a defective round before any label is read, and the
readouts route a repair that training could not perform. These
measurements make a good governor and a poor objective, and
\S\ref{sec:driving} gives the reason.

\subsection{Losses and the gradient-locality guarantee}

For a batch $z_1,\dots,z_B$ (normalized) and a \emph{measured} target scale
$\sigma$:
\[
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{conf}}
= \mathop{\mathrm{mean}}_{(i,j) \notin \mathcal E}
  \Phi\!\left(-\frac{\lVert z_i - z_j\rVert}{2\sigma}\right),
\qquad
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{pres}}
= \mathop{\mathrm{mean}}_i \;
  \mathrm{KL}\!\left(p^{\mathrm{base}}_i \,\middle\|\, p_i\right),
\]
where $\mathcal E$ excludes positive and guarded pairs and $p_i$
($p^{\mathrm{base}}_i$) are temperature-softmax similarity rows under the
tuned (frozen base) model. $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{conf}}$ is the batch estimate of $C(\sigma)$. Its
gradient with respect to a pair distance falls off as
$e^{-r^2/8\sigma^2}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:gradwin}), so pairs farther apart than $\approx 3\sigma$ receive exponentially
little of it. The objective therefore cannot disturb the arrangement of
far-apart pairs, whatever the training schedule. The
test suite enforces this, requiring a near pair to receive more than
$10^3$ times the gradient of an orthogonal pair. $\mathcal
L_{\mathrm{pres}}$ is the neighbor-overlap comparison in differentiable
form --- the explicit statement of ``do not rewrite who is similar to
whom.'' (Implementation note: the chord computation floors $2 - 2c$ before
the square root, because at exactly zero the root's infinite derivative
turns masked entries into $0 \cdot \infty$ NaN gradients.)

\subsection{Mining, protocol, and measured effect}

Batch composition is driven by the measurements in three ways.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Sampling:} items are drawn with weight proportional to
  their per-item collision counts, with a uniform floor so that the
  uncrowded bulk still anchors the geometry.
\item \emph{Negatives:} candidate negatives are drafted from the
  \emph{confusable window} --- the cosine range between the measured
  liftoff, where confusability begins, and the duplicate scale, where
  identity begins (operationalized as $\cos \approx 0.98$ throughout).
\item \emph{False-negative guard:} an anchor's top-$m$ base-model
  neighbors are never used as negatives, because the confusable window
  is precisely where unlabeled true relatives live.
\end{itemize} The protocol is staged.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Diagnose}, and take the cheapest fix that the readouts
  allow: a cone alone calls for a linear post-transform; duplicates call
  for data repair; only clustering that the cone cannot explain calls
  for training.
\item \emph{Train} with $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{task}} + \lambda_c
  \mathcal L_{\mathrm{conf}} + \lambda_p \mathcal L_{\mathrm{pres}}$
  at the measured $\sigma$.
\item \emph{Verify} on a held-out reservoir never seen by mining, and
  never grade with the functional that was optimized.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Closing the loop on a full-scale encoder}
\label{sec:loop}

The synthetic example establishes the mechanism. What remains is whether
the full loop --- measure, build the objective from the measurements,
train, re-embed, re-measure --- operates on a contemporary public encoder
and a million-document corpus, with the measurements adjudicating every
round. We ran it on the case-study corpus (\S\ref{sec:instrument}) and its
$0.6$-billion-parameter instruction-tuned encoder. A fixed, stratified $200{,}000$-document
measurement subset (proportional across the four subsets) was drawn once;
$20\%$ was held out and never touched by mining, sampling weights, or
training. The objective is fully unsupervised. It combines
$\mathcal L_{\mathrm{conf}}$, evaluated at the subset's measured
$\sstar = 0.134$ under the false-negative guard, with
$\lambda_p \mathcal L_{\mathrm{pres}}$ against frozen base-model
references. Batches are composed half from collision-weighted sampling
and half from guarded pairs inside the confusable window of
\S\ref{sec:training}, which here runs from the liftoff at $0.769$ up to
$0.98$. Between rounds, a rule fixed in advance --- the \emph{licensing rule}
--- decides whether to continue: $\sstar$ must not fall, and held-out
neighbor overlap must remain at least $0.90$; that is, tuning may
rewrite at most one held-out neighbor in ten.

Three findings follow from Table~\ref{tab:loop}.

\paragraph{The instrument rejects a misspecified round.}
The first LoRA round compared the tuned model against stored embeddings of
full documents while the trainer embedded token-truncated views. The gradient therefore rewarded reproducing full-document embeddings
from truncated text. The model was learning to imitate longer inputs,
not to separate crowded documents. Training
losses fell throughout, while every held-out readout degraded: $\sstar$
down, cohort collisions up $17\%$, the pocket enlarged. The licensing rule
rejected the round from the held-out measurements alone, before any label
was consulted.

\paragraph{Corrected training is safe but marginal.}
With references matched to the trainer's own views and the confusable
window present in every batch, both LoRA and full fine-tuning leave every
readout intact: held-out overlap stays at $0.97$ and the flagged cohort
improves by $2$--$3\%$ in the median. The effect is small but nearly
universal across items. Of the $408$ flagged items in the held-out split,
$406$ under LoRA and $356$ under the full fine-tune individually reduce
their expected collisions (paired sign tests, $p < 10^{-100}$ and $p <
10^{-56}$). $\sstar$ is unchanged rather than merely noisy: the paired
difference across eight independent pair samples is $+0.00007$ against a
per-sample standard deviation of $0.00012$. This holds even though the
full fine-tune moves roughly $40\times$ the parameters of the LoRA round
on a doubled pair batch.

\paragraph{Capacity is not the binding constraint.}
The linear adapter halved collisions on the synthetic single-pocket
benchmark (Table~\ref{tab:training}), yet the real corpus rejects it:
$\sstar$ falls and cohort collisions worsen by $9\%$, because one linear
map cannot serve the corpus's mixture of scales. LoRA and the full
fine-tune land within noise of one another. The corpus's dominant pocket
is textual near-duplication, and the preservation term refuses to separate
items whose inputs are near-identical. This confirms the staged protocol's
routing rule from the training side: duplicate pathology is a data-repair
problem, and no objective that preserves base-model semantics can
substitute for deduplication. Training at the measured scale is safe, but
it is not a shortcut around the data.

As a final check that the subset verdicts transfer, the full corpus was
re-embedded with the accepted fine-tune and measured at full scale with the
identical pipeline as the base case study. Every readout reproduces within measurement noise: $\sstar$ $0.1227$
against the base $0.1226$; liftoff $0.817$; effective rank $728$;
IsoScore $0.089$; mean pair cosine $0.235$; hubness skew $1.88$; top
pocket $198$ items at prominence $0.090$ against the base's $204$ at
$0.090$. Two things follow. The accepted fine-tune preserves the
geometry at $10^6$ documents, and the $200$k held-out measurement is a
faithful stand-in for the full corpus.

\emph{Reading Figure~\ref{fig:loopfig}.} The left panel shows the
counterfactual that the instrument prevented. Both rejected rounds sit
\emph{above} the baseline --- their tuned models made the flagged cohort
worse --- while their training losses fell monotonically. Loss alone
endorsed those rounds; only the held-out geometric readouts saw the
regression. The right panel shows the gate that caught them: the rejected
LoRA round is the only point below the overlap floor fixed in advance, and
the accepted rounds cluster near the base model's neighborhood structure.
The contribution here is not the marginal improvement of the accepted
rounds. It is that on every round the labels later scored, the held-out
geometric verdict agreed in sign with the label-based evaluation, at no
label cost.

\subsection{What the objective alone does to retrieval}
\label{sec:driving}

The loop above never touches a relevance judgment. Judgments enter once,
after every round is frozen, and only to score predictions that the
instrument had already committed to. On the corpus's
citation-grounded question-to-section retrieval set ($1{,}000$ held-out
questions against a $35{,}173$-section sub-corpus, exact search), the
unsupervised full fine-tune moves recall@1 from $0.288$ to $0.301$ and
MRR@10 from $0.467$ to $0.475$; the LoRA round is flat ($0.286$, $0.466$).
The recall gain does not reach significance at this query count: McNemar's
exact test on the paired outcomes gives $p = 0.45$ ($118$ against $131$
discordant queries), and the bootstrap interval on the MRR@10 difference,
$[-0.008, +0.023]$, spans zero. The honest reading is therefore neutral to positive: the geometry improves
item by item, while the aggregate task effect is bounded near zero. That
is consistent with the duplication finding, since most failures here are
not caused by the crowding this objective is permitted to repair.

For context, a label-supervised
fine-tune measured under the same harness reaches recall@1 $0.355$, an
upper reference point supplied by supervision rather than by geometry.

The per-item layer makes a sharper, item-level
prediction: documents with high base-model expected collisions should be the
ones that \emph{fail at their own queries} (a judged-relevant document
outranked by non-relevant neighbors). Scored blind, base expected-collision counts predict which documents
fail at AUC $0.578$, against $0.551$ for the natural control (top-$1$
neighbor cosine), over $953$ gold documents. The signal is better than chance (bootstrap $95\%$ interval $[0.536,
0.622]$), though the margin over the proximity control is not individually
significant on this single corpus ($+0.028$, paired bootstrap interval
$[-0.017, +0.073]$). \S\ref{sec:external} settles that comparison across
five corpora and seven encoders.

This section has exercised the whole map from readout to intervention:
\begin{itemize}
\item a cone and nothing more calls for a linear post-transform;
\item named near-duplicate pockets route to data repair before any
  training;
\item clumping that the cone cannot explain licenses guarded training at
  the measured $\sstar$, checked on held-out measurements against a rule
  fixed in advance;
\item per-item collision counts rank which documents to repair or
  re-chunk first;
\item $\sstar$ sets the query-noise budget an application may spend.
\end{itemize}
Every entry on that map is a governing action: it decides what to do,
where, and at which similarity level, or it vetoes a step someone else
proposed. None
of them is the objective doing the work by itself. That distinction is
the section's result, and it follows from the mechanism above rather than
from a shortfall of capacity or tuning. A crowding measurement can only
be trained away when the crowding is something training is permitted to
move, and the near-duplicate mass that dominates this corpus is not. The
external grid shows the same limit from the other direction: failures
caused by composition rather than by crowding are invisible to the
per-item field (\S\ref{sec:external}). Used to govern --- to select
the repair, to bound the damage of a supervised objective, to reject a
round --- the measurements pay for themselves; used as the objective,
they do not.

\subsection{Executing the routing rule}
\label{sec:routing}

We tested the routing claim directly, executing the repair that the
measurement prescribed while blind to all labels, then scoring on the
frozen evaluation. The repair has three mechanical steps:
\begin{enumerate}
\item identify near-duplicate groups purely from geometry (pairs above a
  stated cosine threshold, grouped by connectivity);
\item reduce each group to one canonical member, chosen by identifier
  order;
\item map relevance judgments through that reduction mechanically.
\end{enumerate} Two repair sites and two scales give four combinations,
and only one of them is a repair (Table~\ref{tab:routing}).

Removing \emph{foreign} near-duplicates inside the confusable window ---
the band that opens at the liftoff cosine --- fixes $12$ queries and breaks none (McNemar exact
$p = 5 \times 10^{-4}$). This is the retrieval gain that training could
not deliver. Merging \emph{within} the
formulaic target corpus at that same scale is harmful, breaking $57$
queries: these regulatory sections share boilerplate, so their confusable
window holds legally distinct material. Within the corpus, only merging at
the true-duplicate scale ($\cos \ge 0.95$) helps.

The two scales the instrument reports are therefore not interchangeable.
Liftoff marks the onset of confusability rather than the duplicate
boundary, and
each scale licenses a different repair --- a mapping the held-out labels
confirm in both directions (Figure~\ref{fig:routingfig}).

at that same scale mostly breaks. At the true-duplicate scale the pattern

\label{fig:routingfig}
\end{figure}

\emph{Reading Figure~\ref{fig:routingfig}.} ``Deduplicate the corpus''
names a single operation, but its effect depends on site and scale: of the
four cells in the figure, only one is cleanly positive. The measurement
supplied the two numbers that disambiguate the operation --- the liftoff
cosine, where confusion begins, and the duplicate scale, where identity
begins --- and each licenses a different intervention.

\subsection{Guardrails for supervised fine-tuning}
\label{sec:guardrail}

This is the second role: the measurements supplement a label-supervised
objective rather than replacing it. The motivation is a specific failure.
A prior supervised fine-tune of the case-study encoder improved
first-rank retrieval on the task it was trained for, while quietly
collapsing a multi-hop evaluation it was not trained for. That is the
failure class $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{pres}}$ and the false-negative guard
exist to prevent. We ran the identical supervised recipe (in-batch contrastive loss on
$45{,}465$ question--section pairs, two epochs) in two arms differing
only in the guardrails.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Control:} the task loss alone.
\item \emph{Guarded:} the task loss plus $\lambda_p \mathcal
  L_{\mathrm{pres}}$ against the frozen base on identical truncated
  views. In addition, any batch document whose base-model cosine to the
  anchor's positive exceeds the measured liftoff is masked out of the
  contrastive denominator, so the task loss never pushes apart pairs
  that the base model considers confusable relatives.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The gate ranked the arms before any label was read.}
The guarded model leads on every label-free readout: neighbor overlap
$0.650$ against $0.537$, $\sstar$ $0.146$ against $0.136$ (the base being
$0.134$), and flagged-cohort collisions $0.47$ against $2.10$.

\paragraph{The frozen evaluations confirmed the prediction.}
On the targeted task the guardrails cost nothing: MRR@10 is $0.503$
against $0.501$, both far above the base $0.467$. On the untargeted open
evaluation the guarded arm wins every metric, with single-document MRR@10
at $0.597$ against $0.563$. The decisive metric is all-gold@10, the
fraction of multi-hop queries whose every judged-relevant document reaches
the top ten, because that is where the earlier regression appeared. There
the guarded arm scores $0.339$ against the control's $0.223$ --- a $52\%$
relative improvement over $1{,}496$ held-out queries, a gap exceeding five
standard errors. The control reproduces the earlier collapse, while the
guard halves the damage and improves every other number.

Two conclusions follow. Measurement-derived guardrails make supervised
domain adaptation safer at no cost to its gains, and the label-free
held-out gate selects the better model before any label is consulted.

\section{External Validation Across Corpora and Encoders}
\label{sec:external}
% =========================================================================

The evidence so far comes from synthetic benchmarks and one real corpus.
This section tests the instrument on five judged public collections and
seven public encoders (Tables~\ref{tab:corpora}
and~\ref{tab:encoders}). Each combination of a corpus and an encoder is
one \emph{cell} of the grid, treated as its own embedded corpus ---
first measured, then scored. Five corpora under seven encoders give
$35$ cells, plus the legal case study. Each cell follows four steps in strict order:
\begin{enumerate}
\item embed the corpus;
\item compute the full readout set and record its hash, \emph{before} any
  judgment file is opened;
\item compute the per-document collision field of \S\ref{sec:semantics}
  (each document's expected collision count) at the cell's measured
  $\sstar$;
\item only then run and score the queries (nDCG@10, recall@100, and
  per-relevant-document failure labels).
\end{enumerate}
As a pipeline check, BGE-large's score on TREC-COVID reproduces its
published benchmark value. Every cell appears in
Table~\ref{tab:external} and in the figures that follow it.

\emph{Reading Figures~\ref{fig:external}--\ref{fig:pocketgrid}.} In the
scatter row, the claim under test is a slope: if geometric headroom
explained retrieval quality, every panel would rise to the right. Only the duplicate-matching panel rises. There, the conventional
summary (mean pair cosine) points the \emph{wrong} way: in the one
setting where geometry is the task, the popular statistic misleads. The AUC strip asks the same question inside each corpus. There one
encoder and one corpus are fixed, so semantic alignment cannot vary. The
geometric signal is positive in every informative cell except the
multi-hop group, drawn in red.
Together the two views separate two questions. Across encoders,
retrieval quality has two ingredients: the geometry, and whether the
encoder places related texts near each other. Geometry predicts only
where the second ingredient is roughly constant. Within a single
embedding the second ingredient cannot vary, so geometry predicts
failure wherever the failure comes from items being hard to tell apart
--- here, in every informative cell except the multi-hop ones. The prominence strip makes the same point for structure. Whether a
corpus forms pockets depends on the corpus--encoder \emph{pair}: the
spread within a column is as large as the spread across columns. That is why the
instrument reports structure per embedding rather than labeling corpora as
clean or dirty.


\textbf{The per-item claim holds on four corpora and fails on the
fifth.} We call a cell \emph{informative} when its failure label varies: some
judged documents fail at rank $10$ and some do not. A label that is
nearly constant cannot be predicted by anything. The informative cells comprise all seven encoders on each of
four corpora --- duplicate questions, product search, open-domain QA,
and multi-hop QA --- plus the legal case study, giving $29$ in all. In
$26$ of them, expected-collision counts predict which judged-relevant
documents fail at their own queries (sign test
$p \approx 8 \times 10^{-6}$); the per-corpus AUC ranges are the final
columns of Table~\ref{tab:external}. Duplicate questions, product
search, and open-domain QA are positive in every cell, including an AUC
of $0.69$ on product search for the case-study encoder despite an $84\%$
base failure rate. The biomedical collection is excluded as
uninformative by construction rather than by counterexample: with $50$
queries carrying hundreds of relevant documents each, $98\%$ of judged
documents exceed rank $10$ and the failure label saturates entirely.

\textbf{Multi-hop retrieval is where the per-item claim stops.} On
HotpotQA the field predicts failure for four of seven encoders
($0.55$--$0.59$) and sits at chance for the other three
($0.483$, $0.486$, $0.494$). These cells are not saturated --- their
failure rates run from $23\%$ to $58\%$, the most balanced labels
anywhere in the grid --- so the null result is a real one. The explanation is in the task. A multi-hop question can be answered
only by combining two documents. A document can therefore fail at its
own query while sitting in uncrowded space: the failure is not that
something confusable outranked it, but that the answer needed a second
document that the query never reached. Geometry cannot see that. This is the per-item counterpart of the corpus-level scope law below.
Both say the same thing: the instrument measures whether items remain
distinguishable, and that predicts a retrieval failure only when the
failure is caused by items being hard to tell apart. Where relevance
requires composition or reasoning, the measurement is silent, and
reporting it as silent is more useful than reporting a number.

\textbf{The corpus-level claim earns only a narrow scope.} Within one corpus, across the seven encoders, the rank correlation of
$\sstar$ with nDCG@10 reaches $+0.75$ only on duplicate matching, the
one task where relevance \emph{is} geometric proximity. On that same
task the mean-cosine control is \emph{inverted} ($-0.79$). On product search the correlation is near zero ($-0.07$): shopping
relevance mixes attribute matching with intent that the geometry cannot
see. On open-domain QA, multi-hop QA, and biomedical search it reverses
($-0.54$, $-0.75$, $-0.32$): the smallest encoder has the best geometry
and the worst retrieval. The two knowledge-seeking corpora agree, which is what
distinguishes a scope law from a single anomalous corpus. The mechanism is the alignment--uniformity decomposition. Retrieval
quality has two ingredients: semantic alignment (whether the encoder
places related texts near each other) and geometric headroom (whether
the space leaves room to tell neighbors apart).
\textsf{ambit} is unsupervised by construction, so it measures only the
second. $\sstar$ therefore ranks encoders only where alignment is
essentially constant across them, and a semantically weak encoder can
attain excellent geometry by spreading apart documents it does not
understand. The operational conclusion is that corpus-level $\sstar$ is a budget
for one corpus rather than a ranking across encoders. It compares one corpus against itself under encoder
variants, repair, or drift, which is how the guardrail gate used it. For
comparison across encoders, the validated readout is the per-item
collision field above.

\textbf{The two similarity levels, checked against annotated
duplicates.} The duplicate-question collection annotates true duplicates, so pair
cosine alone can be tested as a duplicate detector. Its precision rises
steadily with the threshold: $0.29$ at $\cos = 0.80$, $0.93$ at $0.95$,
and $0.98$ at $0.98$ for the case-study encoder. Its recall at the
duplicate level is low, because most annotated duplicates are
paraphrases that sit in the confusable window. Figure~\ref{fig:dedupf1} plots the ladder. At item level it confirms
the two-level reading that the routing experiment (\S\ref{sec:routing})
turned into opposite repair prescriptions: confusability begins at one
similarity level, and true duplication at a higher one.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
% =========================================================================

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{External validation.} A cross-corpus slice now exists
  (\S\ref{sec:external}): the per-item claim holds in $26$ of $29$
  informative cells and fails on multi-hop questions, and the
  corpus-level claim earned a measured scope law. What remains open is
  breadth: more collections; encoders degraded on purpose, step by step,
  to test whether $\sstar$ tracks the degradation; and independent
  replication. Both scope laws are hypotheses to re-test.
  The alignment--uniformity split predicts that adding an alignment
  probe would restore ranking power on knowledge-limited tasks, but any
  such probe requires supervision that this instrument excludes. The
  multi-hop null makes a sharper and cheaper prediction: the per-item
  field should recover on the subset of multi-hop questions answerable
  from a single document and stay at chance on the rest.
\item \textbf{The objective, retested where it should work.} Training at
  the measured $\sstar$ has been tried on one corpus. That corpus's
  crowding is textual duplication, which a semantics-preserving
  objective must leave in place, and retrieval was unchanged. The prediction that follows is testable
  and cheap: on a corpus whose failures really are caused by items being
  hard to tell apart --- duplicate matching, where the per-item field
  reaches an AUC of $0.74$ --- the same objective should produce a
  measurable retrieval gain. If it does not, the objective should be
  withdrawn as a contribution and the training layer stated purely as a
  governor.
\item \textbf{One untested prediction.} The exact confusion transform
  and its near-equivalent alternative order corpora almost identically,
  but they disagree on one case (Table~\ref{tab:redundancy}): the exact
  form ranks duplicate pathology above diffuse collapse. That
  disagreement is falsifiable and has not been tested at scale.
\item \textbf{Query distributions.} The instrument currently treats the corpus's own documents as stand-ins
  for queries. Accepting an optional \emph{unlabeled} set of real
  queries would replace that assumption with a measured confusion
  between queries and documents, still without using labels.
\item \textbf{Sharper aggregates.} Adding up the per-competitor error
  probabilities (a \emph{union bound}) can only overstate the true
  collision count, never understate it --- but it overstates most where a
  few documents sit near very many others. Gaussian comparison
  inequalities may give bounds from both sides.
\item \textbf{Recoverability.} A useful next readout is how much of the measured crowding a limited
  class of transforms could remove. Computing that fraction, rather
  than predicting it, would separate crowding caused by the embedding
  model from crowding inherent in the data.
\item \textbf{Streaming structure.} The crowding curve can be computed
  from a running summary of a stream; the merge tree cannot, which is why
  the structural layer works on a bounded sample rather than on the whole
  corpus. An approximate hierarchy that could be merged across chunks,
  with error bounds on prominence, would remove that limit.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
% =========================================================================

Crowding is a failure that embedded systems exhibit without raising any
error. Global averages cannot see it, and binned displays cannot be
trusted to measure it. The instrument described
here rests on one commitment applied uniformly: read continuous
quantities at every similarity level, against stated references, and
name the individual documents responsible.

The mathematics is classical --- Ripley's curve, Stolarsky's identity, the
distance to a measure, the condensed cluster tree, a Gaussian tail bound.
The contribution is their selection, calibration, and composition into a
system with stated guarantees and stated scope, traced end to end through
one benchmark whose planted pathology every layer detects, localizes,
converts into an expected error count, and partially repairs. Beyond that benchmark, the per-document score identified the documents
that later failed at their own queries, in $26$ of $29$ testable
pairings; the three exceptions are multi-hop questions, where
composition rather than crowding causes the failure. The measurements
also chose interventions that held up once the labels were read. A
deduplication fixed twelve queries and broke none. Penalty terms halved
the damage a supervised fine-tune did outside its training data. A veto
stopped a training round that its own falling loss endorsed. Each of those governs a decision that someone else's objective would
otherwise make alone. Run as the training objective itself, the same
measurements left retrieval unchanged. That negative result is stated
at the same prominence as the successes, because an instrument's
reliability rests in part on a precise statement of its limits.

\section{Engineering Notes}
\label{app:eng}
% =========================================================================

The implementation is organized so that its asymptotic costs match the
claims of \S\ref{sec:instrument}: one streaming pass over the corpus,
followed by statistics whose cost is independent of corpus size. This
appendix records the implementation decisions behind the measured constants
of Table~\ref{tab:scaling}.

\paragraph{Streaming scan.}
Ingestion is a single sequential pass over raw \texttt{.npy} files
(memory-mapped) or sharded Parquet (chunked decode), in batches of
$5\times10^4$ rows. Each batch of $b$ rows updates the running mean, the
norm moments, and the $d \times d$ second-moment scatter through one
$X_b^{\!\top} X_b$ GEMM, and feeds a bounded reservoir sample. Peak
memory is therefore $O(d^2 + \text{reservoir})$ whatever the corpus
size. The covariance spectrum is extracted once, at the end of the
pass. An optional
approximate mode terminates the pass after a prescribed number of rows and
estimates the spectrum from that prefix; \S\ref{sec:instrument} documents
the failure mode this introduces on sorted shards.

\paragraph{Blocked kernels at reservoir scale.}
No computation materializes an $m \times m$ matrix for reservoir size $m$.
Top-$k$ cosine values (the input to the distance-to-measure field) and
collision counts are computed from $b \times m$ Gram tiles with
partial-selection top-$k$ (\texttt{argpartition}, linear per row, in place
of a full sort). The mutual-reachability minimum spanning tree uses a
vectorized Prim iteration that maintains a frontier-distance array:
$O(m^2)$ arithmetic executed as full-array operations, $O(m)$ memory
beyond the current tile. The structural layer additionally caps its inputs
($6{,}000$ points for the DTM field, $4{,}096$ for the merge tree), which
fixes the corpus-size-independent constants observed in
Table~\ref{tab:scaling}.

\paragraph{Null sampling without vector synthesis.}
All isotropic-null quantities exploit the closed form for the pair cosine
of independent uniform directions on $S^{d-1}$,
$c = 2B - 1$ with $B \sim \mathrm{Beta}\!\left(\tfrac{d-1}{2},
\tfrac{d-1}{2}\right)$: null pair samples are drawn directly from this
scalar law. A $199$-replicate rank envelope therefore requires $199$
scalar-array draws rather than $199$ synthetic corpora. The anisotropy-conditioned
reference uses the rotation invariance of the pair-cosine law under the
angular central Gaussian --- only the covariance spectrum enters --- so
reference pairs are generated from a small pool of spectrum-weighted
Gaussian directions with no eigenvector computation. The Gaussian
confusion law is evaluated with a vectorized rational approximation to
$\operatorname{erf}$ (absolute error below $1.5\times10^{-7}$), which
keeps the core free of dependencies beyond \textsf{numpy}.

\paragraph{One shared pair sample.}
A single random-pair cosine sample (default $2\times10^5$ pairs) is drawn
once per corpus and reused by every pairwise statistic --- $K(t)$, the
envelope test, the discrepancy $z$, the confusion functional $C(\sigma)$,
and $\sstar$. The continuous layer therefore costs one pass over a
fixed-size array per statistic, and all reported statistics refer to the
same measured sample.

\paragraph{Distributed training and pair-batch statistics.}
The encoder-loop trainer (\S\ref{sec:loop}) runs data-parallel: each rank
embeds its own rows and the embeddings are gathered across ranks (the local
slice retaining its gradient) before the pair losses, so pair statistics
scale with world size at fixed per-device memory. This matters beyond throughput. The pair losses see only within-batch
pairs, so small pair batches permit batch-level overfitting: the model
separates the pairs it is shown while degrading structure it never sees.
The loop's held-out measurements catch that failure. Gathered batches must be identically shaped on
every rank; the batch builder emits a fixed count of distinct rows per rank
for this reason.

\paragraph{Optional acceleration tiers.}
The core is \textsf{numpy}-only, with the heavy kernels routed through
multi-threaded BLAS. An optional module provides the same three kernels
--- covariance accumulation, pair cosines, and exact blocked $k$NN --- on \textsf{torch} devices (CPU, CUDA, or Apple \textsf{mps}, with
automatic selection), transferring each chunk to the device as it
streams. An approximate-neighbor path through FAISS is available where
installed.
These tiers change constants rather than asymptotics; every number reported in
this paper was produced by the \textsf{numpy}-only configuration
(Appendix~\ref{app:env}).

\section{The Report Artifact}
\label{sec:report}
% =========================================================================

The toolkit delivers its measurements as a single self-contained,
theme-adaptive HTML report that makes no external requests. Its design is
documented here for reproducibility. The artifact is engineering rather than an
academic contribution: the contribution of this report is the measurement
layer of \S\ref{sec:continuous}--\S\ref{sec:semantics}, exposed as library
functions independent of any rendering.

Two design rules follow directly from the measurement theory.
\emph{Reference curves are comparisons only}: every panel draws its
reference curve,
because absolute cosine is not self-interpreting (\S\ref{sec:geometry}).
\emph{Display versus measurement}: two-dimensional projections lay figures
out but never carry a statistic, because projected density is not corpus
density (\S\ref{sec:binned}).

The remaining choices are presentation craft. One accent color is used,
and meaning is carried by position along annotated axes. Figures are
ordered so that each answers the question the previous one raises:
whether crowding exists, who is affected, and what structure they form.
Every panel carries a short explainer stating the context a reader would
otherwise have to assume --- how the null was built, the subsample
sizes, and where the identifiers come from. One legibility finding is worth recording, because it
reinforces the display/measurement separation. A minimum-spanning-tree view drawn over the projection stayed too busy
to read on real corpora, even when restricted to the tightest bridges,
and became a hidden option. The condensed tree of \S\ref{sec:tree}
shows the same object legibly, as prominence bars.

\section{Test Environments}
\label{app:env}
% =========================================================================

The hardware is incidental to the results, and only three facts about it
bear on a claim in this report.

\begin{itemize}
\item Every reported \emph{measurement} was computed by the released
  \textsf{numpy} core, on Python~3.13 with multi-threaded OpenBLAS. That
  is the configuration the portability claim is about.
\item Embedding, fine-tuning, and the exact per-document collision
  fields at million-row scale ran on separate accelerated hardware --- a
  workstation with four GPUs of $96$\,GB each --- through the artifact's
  optional \textsf{torch} path. Those steps produce inputs to
  measurements, never measurements themselves.
\item No experiment uses code outside the artifact and its stated
  experiment scripts, and random seeds are fixed and stated per
  experiment.
\end{itemize}

\section{Formula Glossary}
\label{app:glossary}
% =========================================================================

Every formula in the report is restated here in plain language: what it
formulates, why it matters, how it is used, and what every symbol and
operation means. Entries follow the order of first appearance. The first two entries
explain the shared notation and the recurring vocabulary; each later
entry still lists all of its own symbols.

\paragraph{How to read the notation (used throughout).}
\begin{itemize}
  \item $n$ --- the number of documents in the corpus. $d$ --- the number
  of coordinates in each embedding vector (the ``dimension'').
  \item $i, j$ --- indices naming individual documents, each running from
  $1$ to $n$. Writing $x_i$ means ``the embedding vector of document number
  $i$''; a pair $(i,j)$ names two different documents.
  \item $\Pr[\,\text{event}\,]$ --- ``the probability that the statement
  inside the brackets is true.'' A subscript names what is random: in
  $\Pr_{(i,j)}[\cdots]$ the random thing is the choice of pair --- draw two
  distinct documents at random, and ask how often the bracketed statement
  holds. With no subscript, the randomness is stated in the entry (for the
  flip law it is the query noise).
  \item $\{\sigma : C(\sigma) \le 1\}$ --- ``the set of all values $\sigma$
  such that the condition after the colon holds''; $\max\{\cdots\}$ takes
  the largest member of a set.
  \item $\sum_{j \ne i}$ --- add up the expression that follows, once for
  every allowed value of the index ($j$ running over all documents except
  $i$). $\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i,j}$ --- add over all $n^2$ ordered pairs,
  then divide by their count: an average over pairs.
  \item $\int f(r)\, dK(r)$ --- an average of $f$ weighted by how often
  each distance $r$ occurs among pairs; ``$dK(r)$'' means ``the fraction of
  pairs whose distance is (approximately) $r$.''
  \item $\E$ --- the expected value (average) over the stated randomness.
  \item $\log$ --- the natural logarithm; $e^{x}$ --- the exponential
  function, its inverse.
  \item $\partial f / \partial r$ --- the derivative of $f$ with respect to
  $r$: how fast $f$ changes when $r$ changes a little. $\propto$ ---
  ``proportional to'': equal up to a constant factor that does not depend
  on the variable under discussion.
  \item $\lVert v \rVert$ --- the Euclidean length of a vector $v$ (square
  root of the sum of its squared coordinates). $\langle u, v \rangle$ ---
  the dot product of two vectors (multiply matching coordinates, add them
  up).
  \item $\Phi$ --- the standard normal cumulative distribution function:
  $\Phi(u)$ is the probability that a standard Gaussian (bell-curve) random
  number falls below $u$. $\Phi(0) = 1/2$; $\Phi(-4) \approx 3 \times
  10^{-5}$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Words used throughout (not formulas).}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{similarity level} --- a value of the cosine at which the
  corpus is examined, $\cos = 0.9$ for instance. ``At every similarity
  level'' means a statistic is read at all of them rather than at one
  chosen cutoff.
  \item \emph{null model} --- a simulated corpus of the same size and
  dimension used as the comparison; no real corpus matches it, and the
distance from it is what the statistics report. Two appear in this
report: points spread as
  evenly as chance allows, and the same with the corpus's cone
  reproduced.
  \item \emph{region} (also \emph{cap}) --- a patch of the sphere: every
  direction lying within some angle of a chosen centre.
  \item \emph{pair share} --- the fraction of all document pairs falling
  inside a group. A group holding a fraction $f$ of the documents holds
  about $f^2$ of the pairs, which is why a small group is diluted in any
  average over pairs.
  \item \emph{pocket} --- a group of documents sitting tightly enough
  together to be reported against the null floor.
  \item \emph{guard terms} --- extra penalty terms built from the
  measurements and added to someone else's training objective, rather
  than replacing it.
  \item \emph{flagged cohort} --- the documents whose base-model
  collision counts mark them as most crowded (the top $1\%$), tracked
  across training rounds to see whether an intervention helped them.
  \item \emph{cell} --- one corpus embedded by one encoder: the unit of
  the external validation suite.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Unit vectors and cosine similarity: $c_{ij} = \langle x_i, x_j
\rangle$, points on $\sphere$.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What it says:} Every document is represented as a
  length-one arrow in $d$-dimensional space, and the similarity of two
  documents is the cosine of the angle between their arrows.
  \item \textbf{Why it matters:} Cosine is the score retrieval systems
  actually rank by, so all geometry in the report is expressed in the same
  units the system uses.
  \item \textbf{How it is used:} Every statistic in the report is a
  function of these pairwise cosines.
  \item \textbf{Term by term:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $x_i$ --- the embedding vector of document $i$, rescaled to
    length $1$ (each of its $d$ coordinates is a number; the whole vector
    is one point in $d$-dimensional space).
    \item $\sphere$ --- the unit sphere in $d$ dimensions: the set of all
    vectors of length exactly $1$. The superscript $d-1$ is the standard
    name for the sphere's surface (a globe in 3-D space has a 2-D surface);
    nothing in the report depends on this convention.
    \item $\langle x_i, x_j \rangle$ --- the dot product of the two
    vectors, which for length-one vectors equals the cosine of the angle
    between them.
    \item $c_{ij}$ --- shorthand for that cosine between documents $i$ and
    $j$: $+1$ means identical direction, $0$ unrelated (perpendicular),
    $-1$ opposite.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Chord distance: $r_{ij} = \lVert x_i - x_j \rVert = \sqrt{2 - 2
c_{ij}}$.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What it says:} The straight-line distance between two
  points on the sphere, computed directly from their cosine.
  \item \textbf{Why it matters:} The query-noise law below is exact in
  chord distance rather than in cosine, so the report converts between the two
  constantly; this identity is the dictionary.
  \item \textbf{Term by term:}
    \begin{itemize}
    \item $x_i - x_j$ --- the vector pointing from document $j$'s position
    to document $i$'s.
    \item $\lVert x_i - x_j \rVert$ --- that vector's length: the ordinary
    straight-line distance between the two points.
    \item $r_{ij}$ --- shorthand for that distance.
    \item $\sqrt{2 - 2c_{ij}}$ --- the same distance computed from the
    cosine alone. It follows by expanding the squared length: $\lVert x_i -
    x_j \rVert^2 = \lVert x_i \rVert^2 + \lVert x_j \rVert^2 - 2 \langle
    x_i, x_j \rangle = 1 + 1 - 2 c_{ij}$.
    \item Reference points: identical items have $r = 0$; perpendicular
    ones $r = \sqrt 2 \approx 1.41$; opposite ones $r = 2$.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The null cosine scale: coordinates $\sim \mathcal N(0, 1/d)$,
typical cosine $\approx 1/\sqrt d$.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What it says:} If points are spread uniformly over a
  high-dimensional sphere, each coordinate behaves like a Gaussian random
  number with variance $1/d$, and the cosine between two random points is
  almost always within a few multiples of $1/\sqrt d$ of zero (at $d =
  768$, about $\pm 0.036$).
  \item \textbf{Why it matters:} This is the baseline against which
  ``suspiciously similar'' is judged: in high dimension, unrelated items
  are \emph{very} nearly perpendicular, so even modest cosines are
  extremely surprising under the null.
  \item \textbf{Term by term:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $\sim$ --- ``is distributed as'': the quantity on the left
    behaves like a random draw from the distribution on the right.
    \item $\mathcal N(0, 1/d)$ --- the normal (bell-curve) distribution
    with mean $0$ and variance $1/d$: values concentrate near zero, spread
    $\approx 1/\sqrt d$.
    \item $1/\sqrt d$ --- the typical size of a null cosine; it shrinks as
    dimension grows, which is the concentration phenomenon.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Pair-closeness curve: $K(t) = \Pr_{(i,j)}[\, c_{ij} \ge t\,]$.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What it says:} For every similarity threshold $t$
  simultaneously, the fraction of document pairs that are at least
  $t$-similar.
  \item \textbf{Why it matters:} It is the report's primary object: a
  complete, bin-free record of how much of the corpus sits at every
  closeness scale. Crowding appears as an excess of very close pairs ---
  the curve ``lifting off'' above its reference at high $t$.
  \item \textbf{How it is used:} Estimated exactly by sorting a large
  random sample of pair cosines; compared against the two null curves
  below; the comparison is tested with the rank-envelope test.
  \item \textbf{Term by term:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $K$ --- the name of the curve (after Ripley's $K$ function in
    spatial statistics, of which this is the spherical, cosine-coordinate
    version). For each input $t$ it returns one number between $0$ and
    $1$.
    \item $t$ --- a similarity threshold, swept continuously from low to
    high; the curve is read at every $t$ at once, which is what ``no bins''
    means.
    \item $(i, j)$ --- a pair of two distinct documents, chosen uniformly
    at random from the corpus.
    \item $\Pr_{(i,j)}[\, c_{ij} \ge t\,]$ --- the probability, over that
    random choice of pair, that the pair's cosine reaches the threshold ---
    equivalently, simply the fraction of all pairs that do.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The uniform null for pair cosines: $c = 2B - 1$ with $B \sim
\mathrm{Beta}\!\big(\tfrac{d-1}{2}, \tfrac{d-1}{2}\big)$.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What it says:} If points were spread perfectly evenly on
  the sphere, the cosine of a random pair would follow this exact, known
  distribution --- a Beta distribution stretched from $[0,1]$ to $[-1,1]$.
  \item \textbf{Why it matters:} It gives the ``perfectly spread corpus''
  reference curve in closed form: no simulation of fake corpora is needed
  (one draws random \emph{numbers} rather than random \emph{vectors}), which is
  what makes $199$-replicate null bands cheap.
  \item \textbf{Term by term:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $c$ --- the pair cosine whose null distribution is being stated.
    \item $B$ --- a random draw from the Beta distribution, which produces
    numbers between $0$ and $1$.
    \item $\mathrm{Beta}(a, a)$ with $a = (d-1)/2$ --- a two-parameter
    family of distributions on $[0,1]$; with both parameters equal it is a
    symmetric bell around $1/2$ that narrows as dimension grows (the
    concentration phenomenon again).
    \item $2B - 1$ --- rescales $[0,1]$ onto $[-1,1]$ so the result is a
    cosine: $B = 1/2$ (the typical draw) maps to $c = 0$, i.e.\
    perpendicular.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The anisotropy-matched reference (ACG): $x = \sqrt{\lambda}
\odot g \,/\, \lVert \sqrt{\lambda} \odot g \rVert$.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What it says:} A second reference corpus that copies the
  real corpus's overall \emph{shape} --- its elongation along some
  directions and flattening along others --- but contains no clustering
  whatsoever.
  \item \textbf{Why it matters:} Learned embeddings almost always occupy a
  narrow cone (the ``cone effect''), which inflates all cosines benignly.
  Comparing the data against this reference, instead of against the
  uniform null alone, separates crowding the cone explains (benign) from
  crowding it cannot (genuine pockets).
  \item \textbf{Term by term:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $g$ --- a vector of $d$ independent standard Gaussian random
    numbers: a direction chosen with no preference at all.
    \item $\lambda$ --- the corpus's covariance eigenvalues: a list of $d$
    non-negative numbers, one per principal direction, each saying how much
    variance the corpus has along that direction. Together they describe
    the corpus's ellipsoidal shape.
    \item $\sqrt{\lambda} \odot g$ --- multiply each coordinate of $g$ by
    the square root of the matching eigenvalue ($\odot$ is coordinate-wise
    multiplication); this stretches the isotropic random cloud into the
    corpus's ellipsoid.
    \item dividing by $\lVert \cdots \rVert$ --- rescale to length one, so
    the reference point lands on the sphere like the real data.
    \item Only the eigenvalues enter --- rotating a corpus changes none of
    its pairwise cosines, so the eigen\emph{vectors} are irrelevant here.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The global rank-envelope test and the liftoff cosine.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What it says:} The test decides whether the data curve $K(t)$ exceeds the null band
  across \emph{all} thresholds at once, rather than at one chosen
  threshold. It simulates many null curves, ranks the data curve among
  them at every threshold simultaneously, and returns one global
  $p$-value. The \emph{liftoff cosine} is the smallest similarity at
  which the data curve exits the envelope, reported only when the global
  test rejects.
  \item \textbf{Why it matters:} Reading exceedance off a pointwise band at
  each threshold separately commits a multiple-comparison error --- on pure
  noise it fires essentially always (measured: every one of $40$ null
  corpora). The global test's false alarms match its nominal rate.
  \item \textbf{How it is used:} Every liftoff claim in the report is gated
  on this global $p$; extreme-rank-length (ERL) ordering breaks ties among
  simulated curves.
  \item \textbf{Term by term:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item envelope --- the band spanned by the simulated null curves
    (here $199$ of them, drawn via the Beta law above).
    \item $p$-value --- the probability, if the corpus were truly
    well-spread, of seeing a curve as extreme as the data's anywhere along
    its length. Small $p$ (e.g.\ $0.005$) means ``a well-spread corpus
    essentially never looks like this.'' With $199$ simulations the
    smallest attainable $p$ is $1/200 = 0.005$.
    \item ERL (extreme rank length) --- the tie-breaking rule: when two
    curves are equally extreme at their worst point, the one that stays
    extreme over a longer stretch ranks as more extreme.
    \item liftoff cosine --- the onset similarity of genuine tight-pair
    structure: below it the data track the reference; above it they exceed
    the envelope.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Stolarsky invariance and the occupancy discrepancy $z$:
$\text{cap discrepancy} = \text{const} - \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{i,j} \lVert
x_i - x_j \rVert$.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What it says:} Take every possible ``cap'' on the sphere
  (every center, every radius), compare how many points each cap holds
  against how many it should hold, square the errors, and add them all up.
  Stolarsky's theorem says this total --- which looks uncomputable ---
  equals a constant minus the average distance between pairs of points.
  \item \textbf{Why it matters:} It is the exact completion of what binned
  occupancy displays approximate: \emph{every} lattice at \emph{every}
  scale simultaneously, with no lattice to choose. A corpus that
  under-spreads (crowds anywhere, at any scale) has a smaller average
  pairwise distance.
  \item \textbf{How it is used:} The report computes the mean pairwise
  chord and standardizes it against the uniform null, giving the occupancy
  discrepancy $z$. On the benchmark, uniform controls score $z \approx 0$
  and the planted pocket scores $z = -47$.
  \item \textbf{Term by term:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item cap discrepancy --- the total squared error between actual and
    ideal cap occupancy, accumulated over all cap centers and radii; the
    formal object on the left of the identity.
    \item const --- an explicit constant that depends only on $n$ and $d$,
    not on where the points are; it plays no role in comparisons.
    \item $\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i,j} \lVert x_i - x_j \rVert$ --- the average
    chord distance over all ordered pairs of documents (sum over every
    pair, divide by the number of pairs).
    \item $z$ --- the reported score: the observed mean chord minus the
    null mean, divided by the null standard deviation, where the null mean
    and standard deviation come from redrawing the same number of pair
    cosines from the Beta law. $z = -47$ reads ``the corpus's average
    distance sits $47$ standard deviations of sampling noise below the
    well-spread value.''
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Distance to a measure: $\dtm_m(x_i) = \big( \tfrac1k
\sum_{j=1}^{k} r_{i,(j)}^2 \big)^{1/2}$ with $k = \lceil m n \rceil$.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What it says:} For each item: the typical distance it must
  reach to gather the nearest $m$-fraction of the corpus around it --- a
  per-item crowding radius. Small radius $=$ crowded; large radius $=$
  isolated.
  \item \textbf{Why it matters:} It moves from corpus-level statistics to
  \emph{named items}, which is what makes findings actionable. It is also
  provably stable: outliers and small perturbations of the corpus move it
  only slightly (the Wasserstein-stability guarantee).
  \item \textbf{How it is used:} Computed for every reservoir item; its low
  tail (the most crowded items) is compared against the uniform corpus's
  band; the report lists the most-crowded and most-isolated items by
  identifier.
  \item \textbf{Term by term:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $\dtm_m(x_i)$ --- the function's name (``distance to the
    measure'') applied to document $i$ at mass fraction $m$; one number
    per document.
    \item $m$ --- the mass fraction, the method's one knob (e.g.\ $0.02$:
    gather $2\%$ of the corpus); being a fraction makes it scale-free in
    corpus size.
    \item $k = \lceil m n \rceil$ --- that fraction converted to a count of
    neighbors; $\lceil \cdot \rceil$ rounds up to a whole number.
    \item $r_{i,(j)}$ --- the distance from item $i$ to its $j$th nearest
    neighbor; the parenthesized subscript $(j)$ means the distances have
    been sorted ascending, so $(1)$ is the closest.
    \item $\tfrac1k \sum_{j=1}^{k} r_{i,(j)}^2$ --- the average of the
    squared distances to the $k$ nearest neighbors.
    \item $(\cdots)^{1/2}$ --- the square root of that average: a
    root-mean-square radius, an average robust to any single neighbor.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The merge tree: mutual reachability, birth, death, prominence.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What it says:} Connect items by their shortest bridges
  (single linkage) under the \emph{mutual-reachability} distance. As the
  connection scale grows, tight groups form and later dissolve into the
  bulk. A pocket's \emph{birth} is the scale at which it first holds
  together, its \emph{death} the scale at which it merges into something
  larger, and its \emph{prominence} $=$ death $-$ birth is how long it
  exists as its own entity.
  \item \textbf{Why it matters:} Prominence replaces every flat clustering
  threshold: a pocket is reported not because it passed a cutoff but
  because it persists across a wide range of scales. A null-calibrated
  prominence floor (the $99$th percentile of prominence produced by pure
  noise at matched size and dimension) suppresses spurious pockets.
  \item \textbf{Term by term:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item single linkage --- build connections by repeatedly merging the
    two closest groups; the merge tree records the order and scale of
    every merge.
    \item mutual reachability of $a, b$ --- $\max\{d(a,b),
    \mathrm{core}(a), \mathrm{core}(b)\}$: the largest of the three listed
    quantities, where $d(a,b)$ is the ordinary distance between the two
    points and $\mathrm{core}(x)$ is the distance from $x$ to its own
    $k$th nearest neighbor. Taking the max means two points count as close
    only if each is also locally dense --- a noise point far from
    everything cannot form a spurious bridge.
    \item birth / death --- the connection scales at which a group first
    assembles and at which it is absorbed into an older group (the
    \emph{elder rule}: when two groups meet, the younger one dies).
    \item prominence --- death minus birth, in distance units: the width of
    the scale range over which the pocket is a separate entity.
    \item excess of mass --- the selection rule choosing, on each branch of
    the tree, the cluster that persists longest.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The query-noise model: $q = x + \sigma g$, $g \sim \mathcal N(0,
I_d)$.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What it says:} A query aimed at item $x$ lands nearby
  rather than exactly on it: $x$ plus Gaussian noise of scale $\sigma$ in
  every direction.
  \item \textbf{Why it matters:} It converts geometry into operational
  risk. Real queries paraphrase, truncate, and typo; $\sigma$ is the
  abstract knob for all such imprecision, and every downstream quantity is
  a function of it.
  \item \textbf{Term by term:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $q$ --- the query vector the system actually searches with.
    \item $x$ --- the item the query ``means'': its intended target.
    \item $g \sim \mathcal N(0, I_d)$ --- a fresh standard Gaussian vector:
    each of the $d$ coordinates is an independent bell-curve draw with mean
    $0$ and variance $1$ ($I_d$, the identity matrix, encodes
    ``independent, unit variance, no preferred direction'').
    \item $\sigma$ --- the noise scale: how far, typically, the query lands
    from its target, in the same units as chord distance. $\sigma = 0$
    means perfect queries; larger $\sigma$ means sloppier ones.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The exact flip law: $\Pr[\langle q, y \rangle > \langle q, x
\rangle] = \Phi(-r / 2\sigma)$.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What it says:} The probability that a noisy query aimed at
  $x$ scores a competitor $y$ higher than $x$ itself depends on nothing but
  the distance between $x$ and $y$, through one evaluation of the normal
  tail. Exactly --- in any dimension.
  \item \textbf{Why it matters:} It is the bridge between geometry and
  retrieval errors, and it explains why near-duplicates are catastrophic:
  as $r \to 0$ the probability approaches $\Phi(0) = 1/2$ --- a duplicated
  document loses half its queries to its twin no matter how small the
  noise is.
  \item \textbf{How it is used:} Verified by direct simulation to
  Monte-Carlo precision (max deviation $9 \times 10^{-4}$); summed over
  competitors to give $C(\sigma)$; used as the training loss kernel.
  \item \textbf{Term by term:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $x$, $y$ --- the query's intended target and one competing
    document, both unit vectors; $q$ --- the noisy query from the previous
    entry.
    \item $\langle q, y \rangle$ --- the dot product of query and
    competitor: exactly the similarity score retrieval ranks by. The
    bracketed statement $\langle q, y\rangle > \langle q, x\rangle$ reads
    ``the competitor scores higher than the intended target,'' i.e.\ the
    retrieval errs.
    \item $\Pr[\cdots]$ --- the probability of that error over the
    randomness of the query noise $g$.
    \item $r$ --- the chord distance between $x$ and $y$; $\sigma$ --- the
    query-noise scale.
    \item $\Phi(-r/2\sigma)$ --- the normal tail evaluated at
    $-r/2\sigma$. Why this argument: the set of points scoring $x$ and $y$
    equally is the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining them,
    which sits at distance $r/2$ from $x$; Gaussian noise of scale $\sigma$
    crosses a boundary $r/2$ away with probability $\Phi(-(r/2)/\sigma)$.
    Larger separation or smaller noise pushes the argument more negative
    and the error probability toward zero.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Expected collisions: $C(\sigma) = \sum_{j \ne i} \Phi(-r_{ij} /
2\sigma) \approx (n{-}1) \int \Phi(-r/2\sigma)\, dK(r)$.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What it says:} For an average item, the expected number of
  wrong competitors that a noisy query would rank above it: add the flip
  probability over every competitor.
  \item \textbf{Why it matters:} The probability that \emph{any} of several events occurs is at most the
  sum of their individual probabilities (Boole's inequality).
  $C(\sigma)$ therefore bounds the probability of \emph{any} retrieval
  error, however the competitors are correlated. The bound errs only on
  the safe side: crowding can make it loose, never wrong.
  \item \textbf{Term by term:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $C(\sigma)$ --- the function's name: expected collision count at
    noise scale $\sigma$; one number per $\sigma$, increasing in $\sigma$.
    \item $i$ --- the item being queried; $j$ --- each competing document
    in turn.
    \item $\sum_{j \ne i} \Phi(-r_{ij}/2\sigma)$ --- the flip probability
    against competitor $j$ (previous entry), added up over all $n-1$
    competitors.
    \item $(n-1) \int \Phi(-r/2\sigma)\, dK(r)$ --- the same sum computed
    from the pair-distance distribution alone: average the flip probability
    over the distribution of pair distances (that is the integral against
    $dK$), then multiply by the number of competitors. This is why a
    random pair \emph{sample} suffices --- no per-item computation is
    needed for the corpus-level curve.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Resolution bandwidth: $\sstar = \max\{\sigma : C(\sigma) \le
1\}$.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What it says:} The largest amount of query noise the corpus
  can absorb before, in expectation, one wrong item outranks the right one.
  \item \textbf{Why it matters:} It is the report's headline operational
  scalar: a noise \emph{budget} in retrieval units. Comparing $\sstar$
  against the same corpus size spread uniformly (the ratio reported
  alongside it) says what fraction of the theoretically available budget
  the embedding retains.
  \item \textbf{Term by term:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $\{\sigma : C(\sigma) \le 1\}$ --- the set of all noise scales at
    which the expected collision count stays at or below one; $\max$ takes
    the largest such scale.
    \item the threshold $1$ --- ``one expected collision per item'': the
    point where retrieval starts, on average, to lose. It is a convention
    (a tolerance), and the report states it wherever $\sstar$ appears.
    \item $C$ is monotone --- more noise always means more collisions ---
    so this largest admissible $\sigma$ is well defined and is found by
    bisection: repeatedly halve an interval known to contain the crossing.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Uniformity as a collision bound: $U_t = \log \E\, e^{-t \lVert x
- y \rVert^2}$ and $C(\sigma) \le (n{-}1)\, e^{U_t}$ at $t = 1/(8
\sigma^2)$.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What it says:} The widely used Wang--Isola ``uniformity''
  loss --- the log of the average of $e^{-t r^2}$ over pairs --- is, at the
  right temperature, exactly the logarithm of a Chernoff upper bound on the
  collision count $C(\sigma)$.
  \item \textbf{Why it matters:} It gives the popular loss operational
  units: its temperature knob $t$ is not folklore but a choice of
  query-noise scale ($t = 2$ corresponds to $\sigma = 0.25$). It also
  explains when uniformity misleads --- it is a bound, and the report
  measures where the bound's ordering and the exact functional's ordering
  disagree.
  \item \textbf{Term by term:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $U_t$ --- the uniformity functional's value at temperature $t$
    (one number per $t$; more negative $=$ more spread).
    \item $x, y$ --- a random pair of documents; $\E$ --- the average over
    that random pair.
    \item $e^{-t \lVert x-y \rVert^2}$ --- a Gaussian-shaped penalty: $1$
    when the pair coincides, decaying with squared distance at rate $t$
    (large $t$ = only very close pairs are penalized).
    \item $\log$ --- the natural logarithm of the average, following the
    original definition.
    \item $t = 1/(8\sigma^2)$ --- the dictionary between temperature and
    noise scale; where the identity holds.
    \item the proof, in one line --- the normal tail bound $\Phi(-z) \le
    e^{-z^2/2}$ (a Chernoff bound) applied to $z = r/2\sigma$ turns each
    flip probability into $e^{-r^2/8\sigma^2}$, and summing gives
    $(n{-}1)\, e^{U_t}$.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The global suite: mean pair cosine, IsoScore, effective rank,
hubness skew.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What it says:} Four whole-corpus summaries used as context
  and as baselines. Mean pair cosine: the average $c_{ij}$ over random
  pairs (how coned the corpus is). IsoScore: how close the covariance is to
  perfectly isotropic --- equal variance in all directions --- on a
  $0$-to-$1$ scale. Effective rank: $\exp$ of the entropy of the
  normalized eigenvalue distribution --- roughly, how many directions carry
  meaningful variance (between $1$ and $d$). Hubness skew: the skewness
  (asymmetry) of the count of how often each item appears in others'
  $k$-nearest-neighbor lists --- heavy right skew means a few ``hub'' items
  intrude into everyone's neighborhoods.
  \item \textbf{Why it matters:} These are the statistics that practitioners
  already consult; the detection-power study shows them blind to small
  pockets that the continuous layer detects at full power, because they
  are global averages.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Training losses: $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{conf}} = \mathrm{mean}_{(i,j)
\notin \mathcal E}\, \Phi\!\big({-\lVert z_i - z_j \rVert}/{2\sigma}\big)$
and $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{pres}} = \mathrm{mean}_i\,
\mathrm{KL}\big(p_i^{\mathrm{base}} \,\|\, p_i\big)$.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What it says:} Two differentiable objectives built from
  the measurements. The first is the batch version of the collision count:
  the average flip probability over batch pairs, at the \emph{measured}
  noise scale, excluding a set of protected pairs. The second pins local
  semantics: for each anchor, the distribution ``who in this batch is
  similar to me'' under the model being trained must stay close to the
  same distribution under the frozen base model.
  \item \textbf{Why it matters:} $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{conf}}$ spends
  gradient only where confusion actually lives, and $\mathcal
  L_{\mathrm{pres}}$ prevents the repair from destroying meaning. Their
  balance ($\lambda_p$) is the loop's central tension: recovery against
  fidelity.
  \item \textbf{Term by term:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathcal L$ --- a loss: a number training tries to make small;
    the subscripts name the two losses (confusion, preservation).
    \item $z_i$ --- the embedding of batch item $i$ under the model
    currently being trained (it changes every step; $x_i$ is reserved for
    the fixed corpus vectors).
    \item $\mathrm{mean}_{(i,j) \notin \mathcal E}$ --- average over all
    ordered batch pairs \emph{except} those in the excluded set; $\notin$
    means ``not a member of.''
    \item $\mathcal E$ --- the excluded pairs: known positives and the
    false-negative guard (each anchor's top-$m$ base-model neighbors,
    which are likely unlabeled true relatives and must not be pushed
    apart).
    \item $\Phi(-\lVert z_i - z_j \rVert / 2\sigma)$ --- the flip law
    applied to the batch pair at the measured scale $\sigma = \sstar$.
    \item $\mathrm{KL}(p \,\|\, q)$ --- the Kullback--Leibler divergence:
    the standard measure of how much a distribution $q$ misrepresents a
    reference distribution $p$; zero exactly when they agree, larger the
    more they differ. The double bar $\|$ is part of the notation and
    separates reference from candidate.
    \item $p_i$ and $p_i^{\mathrm{base}}$ --- for anchor $i$, the softmax
    over its cosines to the rest of the batch (exponentiate each cosine
    divided by a temperature of $0.05$, then normalize to sum to one ---
    low temperature sharpens the distribution toward the nearest
    neighbors), computed under the trained model and under the frozen base
    model respectively.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Gradient locality: $\partial \Phi(-r/2\sigma) / \partial r
\propto e^{-r^2 / 8\sigma^2}$.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What it says:} The derivative of the flip probability with
  respect to pair distance dies off as a Gaussian in $r$: pairs farther
  apart than about $3\sigma$ receive exponentially negligible gradient.
  \item \textbf{Why it matters:} It is a structural safety guarantee:
  minimizing $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{conf}}$ \emph{cannot} disturb the
  corpus's far-pair (dissimilarity) structure, by construction rather than
  by tuning. The test suite enforces it (a near pair receives more than
  $10^3$ times the gradient of an orthogonal pair).
  \item \textbf{Term by term:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $\partial \Phi(-r/2\sigma) / \partial r$ --- how fast the flip
    probability changes as the pair distance $r$ changes: exactly the
    signal backpropagation sends to a pair during training.
    \item $\propto$ --- proportional to: equal up to a constant that does
    not involve $r$.
    \item $e^{-r^2/8\sigma^2}$ --- a Gaussian in $r$: near $1$ for close
    pairs, and collapsing extremely fast once $r$ exceeds a few multiples
    of $\sigma$ --- at $r = 3\sigma$ it is already below $e^{-9/8} \approx
    0.32$ of its peak and falling as the \emph{square} of the distance in
    the exponent.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Neighbor overlap at $k$.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What it says:} For each item, the fraction of its $k$
  nearest neighbors that two embeddings (base and tuned) have in common;
  the report uses $k = 10$ and averages over held-out items.
  \item \textbf{Why it matters:} It is the retrieval-relevant measure of
  ``did the model change who is similar to whom'' --- comparing neighbor
  \emph{identities}, which global similarity statistics can miss --- and it
  is the loop's licensing criterion ($\ge 0.90$).
  \item \textbf{Term by term:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $k$ --- the neighborhood size: how many nearest neighbors each
    item's list contains ($10$ here, matching a retrieval page).
    \item overlap for one item --- $|A \cap B| / k$, where $A$ and $B$ are
    the item's neighbor sets under the two embeddings, $\cap$ is the set
    intersection (members common to both), and $|\cdot|$ counts members:
    $1$ means identical neighborhoods, $0$ fully reshuffled.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The statistical tests used in this report.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{McNemar's exact test} asks whether a model change helped or
  hurt on \emph{paired} outcomes: it looks only at the discordant queries
  (helped by one model, failed by the other --- here $118$ against $131$)
  and asks whether that split is farther from an even division than chance
  alone would produce.
  \item \textbf{The paired sign test} does the same for per-item
  quantities: of $408$ held-out flagged items, $406$ improved --- the probability of a split that uneven under ``no effect'' is vanishingly
  small ($p \approx 10^{-118}$), which is how a tiny median effect can
  still be unambiguous.
  \item \textbf{Bootstrap confidence intervals} quantify uncertainty
  without distributional assumptions: resample the data with replacement
  many times, recompute the statistic each time, and read the spread; an
  interval containing zero means the sign of the effect is not established.
  \item \textbf{AUC} (area under the ROC curve, equivalently the
  Mann--Whitney statistic) measures a predictor's ability to rank: the
  probability that a randomly chosen failing document scores higher on the
  predictor than a randomly chosen non-failing one --- $0.5$ is chance,
  $1.0$ is perfect ordering.
\end{itemize}
