where a space loses resolution — and which items.
The context you need before the report makes sense: what crowding is, why it costs you, and the geometry ambit measures. A walk from first principles, sparse and from the ground up.
the problem

Crowding is a loss of resolution

An embedding model is only as useful as its ability to keep distinct things distinct. When unrelated items land at nearly the same direction, every metric built on that geometry — cosine, nearest-neighbor retrieval, clustering, dedup — inherits the ambiguity.

The space has high nominal capacity (say 768 dimensions) but low effective resolution in practice. The tell is simple: take many random pairs and read their cosine. Healthy spaces keep unrelated pairs near zero; a crowded space scores every pair high, so the signal you care about barely rises above the floor.

Distinct is not only unrelated. A set of genuinely related items packed too tightly is a harm too — you lose the resolution to tell which of several relevant items is best. ambit measures crowding as local density: relatedness-agnostic, unsupervised.
the core axis

Isotropy vs anisotropy

Isotropy (healthy): variance is spread across directions; the cloud fills the space; random pairs are ~orthogonal. Anisotropy (the crowding you observe): the cloud concentrates into a narrow cone, sharing a dominant direction, so items look similar regardless of meaning. Drag either cloud to rotate it.

the geometry, in one line

A shared part swamps the residual

Write each embedding as a shared component plus an item-specific residual, xi = μ + ri. The cosine between two items is

cos(xi, xj) = (μ+ri)·(μ+rj) / ‖μ+ri‖ ‖μ+rj‖

When the shared part μ (or a few dominant directions) is large next to the residuals, the numerator is dominated by μ·μ for every pair — so all cosines inflate toward one common value. That is the cone.

The information you want lives in the residuals ri, but it is buried under the shared direction. The same picture explains why ambit reads cosine against an isotropic reference rather than a fixed number.
why it matters

Low resolution caps everything built on top

Crowding silently lowers the ceiling of every downstream task — and it does so quietly, because the embeddings still look fine.

  • Retrieval / RAG — a compressed similarity range puts the right item barely above the wrong ones; top-k gets noisy and thresholds stop meaning anything.
  • Clustering & dedup — crowded regions merge distinct entities; boundaries blur.
  • Monitoring & drift — if the baseline is already a cone, there is little headroom to detect a real distributional shift.
  • Model selection — effective dimensionality is a label-free predictor of downstream quality, so resolution can rank encoders before you have task labels.
the metrics · occupancy

How much space, and how anisotropic

Each scalar reads against an isotropic reference — the value an even cloud of the same dimension would give — never a fixed threshold.

Mean random-pair cosine anisotropy
The anisotropy fingerprint: the average cosine of unrelated pairs. ≈ 0 is healthy; mass shifted toward 1 is a cone. For iid points on a unit d-sphere the reference is ≈ N(0, 1/d).
IsoScore utilization
How uniformly variance fills the space, in [0,1]. → 1 means the space is fully used; low means most of the nominal capacity sits idle.
Effective rank / participation ratio collapse
Continuous effective dimensionality from the covariance spectrum. Far below the nominal dimension → dimensional collapse — the cloud lives on a thin subspace.
Uniformity spread
Wang–Isola spread on the hypersphere, a single label-free scalar — more negative = more uniform. It reports spread only; it makes no claim about whether the right things ended up together.
Unsupervised by design. ambit reports uniformity, not alignment: uniformity is readable from the embeddings alone, while alignment needs known positive pairs. No labels, no gold pairs.
the metrics · resolution

How distinct, item by item

Occupancy describes the whole cloud; these describe how well it resolves a single neighborhood — the currency retrieval actually spends.

NN cosine margin within-neighborhood
Per item, cos(top-1) − cos(top-2): how much the best match beats the runner-up. A thin margin means the ranking is fragile even when the neighbors are all on-topic.
Hubness (k-occurrence skew) retrieval
How lopsided the "is somebody's neighbor" count is. High skew = a few hubs absorb most queries while anti-hubs are nobody's neighbor — the retrieval-side fingerprint of crowding.
Separability partition
Does a partition occupy distinct regions? Centroid-cosine matrix + kNN purity + silhouette / Fisher. Works on provided labels or ambit's own discovered clusters — for which it adds stability and a modal count.
Local density field multiscale
Per item, how concentrated its neighborhood is, read at several scales. Localizes crowded pockets a global scalar hides — one mode near the reference is roomy; a separated high mode is a pocket.
Local, not just global. A single global number is non-collapsible over clusters — a globally even cloud can be a union of locally collapsed cones. The density field localizes which neighborhoods are crammed.
local vs global

A globally even cloud can hide local cones

Anisotropy is often a global artifact masking local structure: a space can be roomy overall and crammed inside a sub-population. A single global scalar is non-collapsible over clusters — average the parts and the pocket disappears.

So ambit reads the distribution of local concentration at several scales, calibrated against a density-matched isotropic reference. The dandelion is globally a sphere; some of its tufts are tight cones.

Read both scales. "Crowded globally" and "useful locally" can coexist — and the reverse. A trustworthy diagnostic localizes the pocket instead of scoring the whole space at once.
comparison · the same items, two ways

Local neighbor-overlap vs global CKA

A second, relational question: take one dataset embedded two ways — two encoders, or two configurations of one model, aligned by id — and ask how much, and where, the two representations differ. This compares geometries, not coordinates: the two spaces need not even share a dimensionality.

Local — neighbor overlap
Per item, the fraction of its top-k neighbors shared between the two graphs. Retrieval-relevant and dimension-agnostic: it compares neighbor sets, and lights up exactly the items whose neighborhoods were rewritten.
Global — CKA
One similarity over the centered second-moment structure of all pairs. Invariant to rotation, scale, and permutation — it asks whether the two spaces encoded the same overall structure, but is blind to which items moved.
They can disagree informatively: high CKA + low overlap is a re-skin that preserves gross structure while reshuffling fine neighborhoods. ambit pairs them with MMD, Procrustes, a drift field, and a distance-distribution shift.
hubness · reciprocal kNN

Suppress the hubs, read the real structure

In crowded high-dimensional spaces a few points become the nearest neighbor of disproportionately many queries — hubs — while others are nobody's neighbor — anti-hubs. Hubness degrades kNN retrieval and is a practical fingerprint of poor resolution. ambit reports the k-occurrence skew.

--mutual-knn recomputes every neighbor view on the reciprocal (mutual) graph — keeping only edges both points agree on. A hub's surplus one-sided connections drop away, so genuine local structure is read apart from the hubness artifact.

One switch, every view. The reciprocal filter applies to the kNN graph that the density field, separability, and the comparison all read — so the whole report is told without the hub.

design philosophy

Direction, not hue. Reference, not threshold.

Three commitments that hold across every figure in the report.

Good/bad by direction

Meaning lives in which way along an axis, not in a colour. The accent is one hue; "good" and "bad" are positions.

Read against a reference

Cosine is judged against the isotropic distribution of the same dimension — never an absolute threshold, because absolute cosine is not self-interpreting.

Unsupervised

No labels, no gold pairs. ambit measures spread (uniformity), which the embeddings carry on their own; alignment, the supervised half, is out of scope.

measuring occupancy · counting

Counting in boxes

Occupancy is usually measured by laying a lattice over the space and counting what falls in each cell. Shift the lattice half a cell — same cells, same data — and the peak count moves by 30%.

The answer belongs to the lattice, not the data. Cell size and placement are both arbitrary, and both change the number — the modifiable-areal-unit problem, which no clever grid escapes.
measuring occupancy · all boxes at once

Every box, at every scale

So stop picking one. For a cap — a direction plus a size — score the gap between the count inside and what an even spread would put there. Do it for every cap, at every size, and average. That average has a closed form:

avgcaps (count − expected)2 = c − mean ‖xi − xj‖

the mean pairwise distance between points — Stolarsky's invariance principle, 1973.

All boxes at all scales, as one number, with no lattice left to choose.
measuring occupancy · the curve

The crowding curve

Take every pair's distance and plot the fraction closer than θ. No bins — it is an exact ECDF, defined at every scale at once.

On the unit sphere the well-spread reference is exact and razor-thin: its spread shrinks like 1/√d, so in high dimension even a small excess of close pairs is unmistakable.

Where the curve lifts above the reference is the scale at which crowding lives — read off, not assumed.
measuring occupancy · the ruler

A ruler at every entity

Distance-to-measure: for each entity, the radius it needs to gather a fixed share of the corpus. Small means crowded, large means isolated.

Continuous, and per entity — it names which items are crowded, not which cell is full.

And provably stable: nudge the data a little and the score moves a little. A bin count offers no such guarantee — one point crossing one edge moves it by a whole unit.
measuring occupancy · the tree

The family tree of crowding

Join entities by their shortest bridges — a minimum spanning tree — and watch groups merge as the scale grows.

Every tight group gets a birth and a merge. The span between them is its prominence, and no threshold was chosen to get it.

The tree answers how many over-tight pockets exist, how tight each one is, and which entities belong to each.
measuring occupancy · what it buys

Why retrieval cares

A retrieval neighborhood is a cap on the sphere. Two items inside the same small cap are a future collision — a query cannot tell them apart.

So the geometry and the failure are the same object, measured two ways.

The curve gives the scale of the collisions, the ruler names the entities at risk, and the tree says whether the crowding is one broad pile or many tight duplicate pockets.
