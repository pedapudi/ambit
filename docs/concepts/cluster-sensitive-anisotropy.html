ambit — cluster-sensitive anisotropy

ambit / note theme

localized anisotropy

An embedding only earns its keep if items you can tell apart stay apart, and items that belong together stay close. “Anisotropy” is the usual word for when that breaks. What follows is a slow walk from first principles to a measure of where it breaks. Four parts: the stage · why a global number lies · going local · the test case and the proposal.
the problem

You have a dataset embedded by some model, and one question decides whether retrieval works: can the space actually keep its items apart — and where it can’t, which items? The off-the-shelf answers are single global numbers — mean cosine, IsoScore, effective rank — taken over the whole cloud. But a dataset is rarely uniform. One average can call a space healthy while a sub-population has collapsed into a crowded cone, or call it broken when only a couple of cluster directions are stretched. We want a measure that is local — that points at the offending region and says how badly it fails — and that is honest about where geometry stops and the task takes over.

part i · the stage

01What we actually wantthe goal

The use-case is retrieval: a query comes in, you rank everything by cosine, the top results should be the genuinely related ones. Two things have to be true at once, and they pull in opposite directions.

  • Resolution. Items that are not the same should stay far enough apart in cosine to be told apart — unrelated items at low cosine so impostors don't crowd the top of the list, and the genuinely related items inside a neighborhood spaced enough that you can still rank which of them is the best match.
  • Recall. Related items should sit at higher cosine than unrelated ones, so the nearest-neighbor search actually finds them.

So the target is not “make all cosines small” (that destroys recall) and not “make all cosines large” (that destroys resolution). It is a gap: a related-pair distribution sitting high, an unrelated-pair distribution sitting low and well resolved, with daylight between them. Everything below is about one failure mode of that gap — crowding, where a region of the space squeezes items together so cosine can no longer separate them. This costs resolution whether or not the squeezed items are related: unrelated items crammed together is the obvious failure (impostors crowd the top), but a genuinely related cluster packed too tightly loses its internal resolution too — you can no longer rank which of several relevant items is the best match, tell near-duplicates from distinct-but-related neighbors, or order a cluster's own members. ambit's field is relatedness-agnostic by construction: it is unsupervised, so the local density counts whatever sits in a neighborhood, related or not — high local density is lost resolution either way. And crowding is usually local, while our instruments are global.

The whole thing as a budget. The sphere gives a finite amount of angular room. Isotropy is how much of that room is in play; clustering spends it to pull related items together (that buys recall — the search finds them); resolution is the room left over to keep items apart inside a neighborhood (that buys precision — you can rank them and keep impostors out). The two failures are the two ends of this budget: spend too little on grouping and relatives scatter out of the top-k (recall dies); spend too much and a neighborhood collapses below the \(1/\sqrt d\) floor so its items can no longer be ranked (resolution dies). This is why “more isotropic” is not the goal — a perfectly uniform space has maximal resolution and no grouping at all. Good geometry is structured: clusters spread over the sphere (a rich, isotropic arrangement, \(\Sigma_{\text{between}}\)) that stay internally roomy (\(\Sigma_{\text{within}}\) small but not collapsed) — not one global cone (room wasted everywhere) and not over-tight groups (room overspent within). The crowding this note studies is the second failure; §04 onward is about finding it locally.
02The sphere, and the conedirections

L2-normalize and an embedding is a pure direction — a point on the unit sphere \(S^{d-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^d\). Cosine similarity is the dot product, the cosine of the angle between two directions. Length is gone; only orientation remains. Three words, defined geometrically:

isotropic
directions spread evenly over the whole sphere. Two random items are almost perpendicular, \(\cos\approx 0\). Lots of room; high resolution.
anisotropic — the “cone”
directions pile into a narrow angular patch. Even unrelated items share a high cosine. No room; low resolution.

The pathology you have read about in language models — the representation-degeneration / cone effect (Gao 2019; Ethayarajh 2019) — is exactly the second picture: the whole cloud collapses toward one cone, so cosine becomes nearly useless because everything is similar to everything. The question is how to quantify “how cone-like is this dataset” — and, crucially, to do it when the dataset is a mix of crowded and roomy regions rather than a single uniform cloud.

03High dimensions are not your intuitionconcentration of measure

Before any measure makes sense you have to feel how strange the sphere is in 768 dimensions. Pick two points uniformly at random. Their cosine is a random variable with mean \(0\) and standard deviation

\[ \operatorname{sd}(\cos) \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}. \]
where\(\operatorname{sd}(\cos)\) — the spread (standard deviation) of the cosine between two random items\(d\) — the embedding dimension (768 here); larger \(d\) ⇒ a tighter spike at 0

For \(d=768\) that is \(0.036\). So two random items are near-orthogonal with overwhelming probability — the distribution of random-pair cosine is a needle-thin spike at \(0\). This is concentration of measure: almost all of the sphere's area sits near the equator of any fixed point. The figure shows the spike sharpening as \(d\) grows.

Three consequences that drive everything after:

  • “Similar” is a tiny window. If the baseline is \(0\pm0.036\), then a pair at \(\cos = 0.3\) is already eight standard deviations out — wildly similar. Crowding doesn't need cosines near \(1\); it just needs them well above the orthogonal floor.
  • A cluster has to be very tight to exist at all. Add Gaussian noise of size \(\sigma\) around a center; the noise has length \(\sigma\sqrt d\). To stay near the center you need \(\sigma\ll 1/\sqrt d\). A “loose” cluster in 768-d is indistinguishable from background — a fact that quietly sabotages naïve experiments (and bit the first draft of the test case below).
  • The average is owned by the background. Because almost every pair is a near-orthogonal background pair, a mean over pairs — or a covariance summed over points — is dominated by the diffuse majority. A small, intensely crowded pocket contributes almost nothing to the average. This is the seed of every failure of a global number.

part ii · why a global number lies

04The dandelionlaw of total covariance

Picture a dandelion: many tight, dense clusters — each a narrow cone of directions — pointing outward every which way. Each cone is locally crowded: within it, cosine is near \(1\), no resolution at all. But dense is not the same as collapsed — a tight cone is a small round blob in its own tangent space, not a \(1\)-D line; whether a cluster is also low-rank is the separate question §10 settles with IsoScore\*. The global picture then turns on how many cones there are. When they are numerous enough to fill the directions (more cones than dimensions, tips spread all around), their arrangement averages to a round \(\Sigma_{\text{between}}\) and the unit vectors cancel to a near-zero mean — so a global covariance reports isotropic and mean cosine reports \(\approx 0\), “all good,” while every neighborhood is in fact crowded. (“Dandelion” is our handle; the geometry is a union of cones, and the within-cluster tightening is the local half of neural collapse: Papyan, Han and Donoho 2020.)

The bookkeeping is the law of total covariance. Split the points into clusters \(C_k\) with means \(\mu_k\), weights \(w_k=n_k/n\):

\[ \Sigma_{\text{total}} \;=\; \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_k w_k\,\Sigma_k}_{\Sigma_{\text{within}}}\;+\;\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_k w_k(\mu_k-\mu)(\mu_k-\mu)^{\!\top}}_{\Sigma_{\text{between}}}. \]
where\(\Sigma_{\text{total}}\) — covariance of the whole cloud (what a global measure sees)\(\Sigma_k\) — covariance of cluster \(k\) in its own frame (centered on its own mean)\(w_k = n_k/n\) — cluster \(k\)'s weight: its size \(n_k\) over the total \(n\)\(\mu_k,\ \mu\) — cluster \(k\)'s mean direction, and the global mean\(\Sigma_{\text{within}}\) — the pooled within-cluster shape (the average local cloud)\(\Sigma_{\text{between}}\) — covariance of the cluster means (how the clusters are arranged)

For the dandelion, \(\Sigma_{\text{between}}\) is the arrangement of the cone tips and \(\Sigma_{\text{within}}\) is the pooled cone shape (small, because the cones are tight). Two conditions make \(\Sigma_{\text{total}}\) come out round. First, the cones must be numerous. \(\Sigma_{\text{between}}\) is a sum of \(K\) rank-one terms, so it has rank at most \(K-1\): with few cones it is itself anisotropic and \(\Sigma_{\text{total}}\) has effective rank \(\approx K\) — a global number already sees that something is structured. Only once \(K\) exceeds the dimension, tips spread all around, does \(\Sigma_{\text{between}}\) fill out to isotropic. Second, each \(\Sigma_k\) is dense but round in its tangent, not a \(1\)-D collapse. When both hold, the global isotropy is accidental — it lives in the between-cluster arrangement, and the local crowding (high within-cluster density) leaves no trace in the spectrum.

The trap inside the trap. “Then just measure \(\Sigma_{\text{within}}\).” But \(\Sigma_{\text{within}}\) is the pooled (averaged) cluster covariance, and the tight cones — small and pointing every which way — average to a small round blob, which reads isotropic too. The crowding survives only if you look at each cluster's own neighborhood and keep the extremes, never the pooled average. Averaging is the disease; you can't cure it with another average.
05A Simpson's paradox of isotropynon-collapsibility

This has a precise classical name. Simpson's paradox is when a relationship in aggregated data reverses once you condition on a lurking group variable — the marginal is a confounded mixture of the conditionals. The mean version rests on the law of total expectation, \(\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mathbb{E}_Z\,\mathbb{E}[Y\mid Z]\). Here both moments matter — mean cosine is a first moment and IsoScore a second — so both the law of total expectation and the law of total covariance (§04) apply, with the lurking variable being cluster membership.

where\(Y\) — the quantity being averaged (here a cosine / geometry statistic)\(Z\) — the lurking group variable (here, cluster membership)\(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid Z]\) — the within-group (conditional) average\(\mathbb{E}[Y]\) — the aggregate (marginal) average — a confounded mixture of the conditionals

Both global readings are non-collapsible — neither is a weighted average of the per-cluster values, and each can flip in either direction — but by different routes. IsoScore is a nonlinear functional of the covariance (the law of total covariance). Mean cosine is a first-moment quantity, \(\lVert\bar\mu\rVert^2\) (the law of total expectation): it reverses through the mixture of cluster means, not the covariance.

  • Aggregate anisotropic → strata isotropic. Cai et al. (2021): the famous BERT “cone” is mostly the spread of cluster means; center each cluster and it's nearly round. Condition on the cluster and the anisotropy evaporates.
  • Aggregate isotropic → strata crowded. The dandelion (once the cones are numerous enough to fill the directions): the round ball is the arrangement of dense — though individually round — cones. Condition on the cluster and the crowding the aggregate hid reappears.

The cleanest instance is a sign reversal on cosine itself. Take two tight clusters on opposite sides of the sphere. Within each, items are strongly aligned; across them, strongly opposed; and the aggregate — which is mostly cross-pairs — averages to nothing:

So “is this space anisotropic?” has no answer until you say at what stratification. The lesson is identical to Simpson's: never trust a marginal; condition on the grouping first. The measure we build is precisely the stratified analysis, and §04's identity is the device that tells you when the aggregate will lie.

06Three global scalars, three blind spotswhy they disagree

Why not just trust IsoScore? Because the standard global scalars are different nonlinear readings of the same spectrum, and they fail differently. Let \(\lambda_1\ge\dots\ge\lambda_d\ge 0\) be the covariance eigenvalues.

  • Mean random-pair cosine (Ethayarajh). A first-moment average over pairs. The diffuse near-orthogonal majority owns it (§03), so a small crowded pocket barely moves it. Blind to localized crowding.
  • Effective rank \(=\exp(H)\), the exponential of the entropy of the normalized singular values (Roy–Vetterli). Entropy-based, so it answers “how many directions share the variance.” A handful of elevated eigenvalues among 768 barely dents the entropy. Blind to a few dominant axes.
  • IsoScore (Rudman). The \(L_2\) distance of the eigenvalue profile from \(\alpha I\). Hypersensitive to a few outlier eigenvalues — two big ones tank it. So it reacts to a couple of dominant axes, but it cannot tell you whether those axes are a genuine global collapse or just two cluster means sitting far apart (a between-cluster fact). Confounds within-crowding with between-arrangement.

You will see all three on the same data in §11, returning three different verdicts. The point is not that one is “right” — it is that a single global functional of the spectrum throws away the one thing you need: where the variance lives on the sphere.

What clustering is — and isn't — here. Clustering is a sufficient cause of anisotropy, not a necessary one: a single elongated blob, or a few high-variance “rogue” dimensions, is anisotropic with no groups at all. When clustering is the story, it enters the covariance through the within / between split (§04). And a global covariance scalar is fully blind to local crowding only in the many-cluster regime — with few clusters it already shows a low effective rank (\(\approx\) the number of clusters), so it is non-specific (it cannot say which cluster or where) rather than blind. Note too that a detected dense pocket is a tight cluster; a loose, spread-out group is low-density and never registers — and in \(\sim\!768\)-d a loose cluster is anyway indistinguishable from background (§03).

part iii · going local

07The resultant vectorthe primitive

Drop to a single group of unit vectors \(\{x_i\}\) — a cluster, or just an item's nearest-neighbor ball. The natural object is the resultant \(s=\sum_i x_i\) and the mean resultant length \(\bar r=\lVert s\rVert/n\in[0,1]\) (the von Mises–Fisher notion of concentration). It answers “how aligned is this group?”

  • All pointing one way (tight cone): \(s\approx n\,u\), so \(\bar r\approx 1\).
  • Spread over the sphere: the sum random-walks and cancels, \(\lVert s\rVert\approx\sqrt n\), so \(\bar r\approx 1/\sqrt n\approx 0\).

And the identity that makes it cheap and meaningful — the mean within-group cosine is the squared resultant length:

\[ \frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\ne j}\langle x_i,x_j\rangle \;=\; \frac{\lVert s\rVert^2-n}{n(n-1)}\;\approx\;\bar r^{\,2}, \]
where\(x_i\) — the unit vectors (directions) of the items in the group\(n\) — the number of items in the group\(\langle x_i,x_j\rangle\) — the cosine between items \(i\) and \(j\)\(s=\sum_i x_i\) — the resultant: the vector sum of the directions, with length \(\lVert s\rVert\)\(\bar r=\lVert s\rVert/n\) — the mean resultant length (0 = spread out, 1 = perfectly aligned)

since \(\sum_{i,j}\langle x_i,x_j\rangle=\lVert\sum_i x_i\rVert^2=\lVert s\rVert^2\). No \(n^2\) pairwise matrix — just sum the vectors, \(O(nd)\). Call this group's number \(c\). For an isotropic group \(\mathbb{E}[c]=0\) at every size; for a group drawn from a space with mean pair cosine \(\kappa_0\), \(\mathbb{E}[c]\approx\kappa_0\). Crowding is the length of the average vector.

08Two confounds: size and breadthcalibration

Raw \(c\) is not yet comparable across groups. Two geometric corrections:

Size is a random walk, not a bias. The expected concentration of an isotropic group is \(0\) at every size — but its fluctuation is \(\propto 1/\sqrt n\). A small group can random-walk to a respectable \(c\) by luck. So a group's surprise must be judged against a size-matched spread \(\sigma(n)\), not an absolute cosine. (A 10-step walk wanders relatively further than a 10,000-step one.)

Breadth sets the yardstick. “Crowded” only means anything relative to how broad the ambient space is. If the whole model already lives in a cone (\(\kappa_0\) large), every group looks crowded in absolute cosine — the model's fault, not a local anomaly. So measure \(c\) against the breadth: \(\kappa_0\) and \(\sigma(n)\) estimated from the dataset at large, or — this is what an embedding function buys you — from the model's range on a generic reference corpus. Standardize:

\[ z \;=\; \frac{c-\hat c(n)}{\sigma(n)}, \]
where\(c\) — the group's measured concentration (its mean within-group cosine, from §07)\(n\) — the group's size\(\hat c(n)\) — the concentration a same-size group from the ambient breadth would show (the baseline)\(\sigma(n)\) — the size-matched spread of that baseline (the random-walk scale)\(z\) — the standardized, signed surprise: \(z\gg0\) a crowded cone, \(z\ll0\) an over-dispersed pocket

signed, so it speaks in both directions: \(z\gg 0\) is a crowded cone, \(z\ll 0\) is an over-dispersed pocket.

09Against what, exactly?the calibration trap

Here is the choice that is easy to get wrong, and that the test case in §11 exposes. What do you standardize against?

  • Against peer clusters (is this cluster odd among the clusters?). Great when one cluster is sick amid many healthy ones — the sick one is the outlier. But if every cluster is crowded and the roomy part is unclustered background, the clusters are each other's peers and the median/MAD cancels them all. The very thing you want flagged becomes “typical.”
  • Against the breadth (is this group tighter than the ambient space at this size?). A random scoop of a mostly-diffuse dataset is diffuse, so genuinely dense pockets stand out and the background does not. This is the calibration that survives the mixture in §11 — and it's what “the range of the state space” was always pointing at.

So the reference is the breadth, not the peers. (Peer-comparison is a fine secondary view when clusters come from provided labels and you expect them to be alike.)

10Cone vs. ball — what cosine can't seeisoscore*

Mean within-group cosine conflates two different shapes, both of which read as “crowded”:

a cone
genuinely 1-D, directionally collapsed. High mean cosine and a degenerate covariance.
an offset ball
round, but far from the origin. High mean cosine too — its points all point roughly one way from the origin — yet its centered covariance is perfectly isotropic.

For retrieval both are crowded (items hard to rank), so \(c\) is the right headline. But to know why — directional collapse vs. mere offset — read the group's centered covariance shape: IsoScore on \(\Sigma_k\), which asks how far the eigenvalue profile is from all-equal.

Why IsoScore\*, not plain IsoScore. A group usually has fewer points than dimensions (\(n_k\lt d=768\)), so \(\Sigma_k\) is rank-deficient — at most \(n_k-1\) nonzero eigenvalues. Naïve IsoScore counts all the forced-zero directions and screams “anisotropic” for every small group. IsoScore\* (Rudman and Eickhoff 2024) fixes this with shrinkage — blend \(\Sigma_k\) toward an isotropic reference while keeping the full \(d\): \[ \Sigma_\zeta \;=\; (1-\zeta)\,\Sigma_k \;+\; \zeta\,\tfrac{\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_k}{d}\,I, \qquad \zeta\in[0,1). \] Because the target is a scaled identity it shares eigenvectors with \(\Sigma_k\), so the blend is exact in eigenvalue space — it just lifts the forced-zero floor by \(\zeta\,\overline\lambda\). A genuinely round small group then reads round; only a group that has truly collapsed onto a low-dim sliver keeps the lopsided eigenvalues that read anisotropic. (We set \(\zeta\) to the rank-deficiency fraction \(1-(n_k-1)/d\): zero once \(n_k-1\ge d\) — where plain IsoScore is already reliable — and larger as the sample starves the dimensions. The paper learns \(\zeta\); shrinkage, the paper stresses, is the crucial part.)

part iv · the test case and the proposal

11Worked example: a uniform background + two dense clustersthe test case

The distribution you posed: ~90% of items uniform on the sphere, plus two tight, mutually-distant dense clusters (5% each). Synthetic, \(d=768\), 10,000 items. Here is what each lens says.

isoscore0.30  reads anisotropic
effective rank750 / 768  reads isotropic
mean pair cosine+0.004  reads isotropic
dims for 90% var634 / 768  reads isotropic

Three global scalars, three verdicts. IsoScore drops (it reacts to the two cluster-mean axes via its \(L_2\) sensitivity), while effective rank stays near full (two raised dimensions among 768 barely touch the entropy) and mean cosine averages the clusters away to zero. None of them localizes, and IsoScore's number would be misread as “the space is collapsing” when 90% of it is perfectly isotropic. This is §05 and §06 made concrete.

The local field settles it. Each item's neighborhood concentration is sharply bimodal: a diffuse mode (the background, resolved) and a separate crowded mode, with an empty valley between. The mass above the valley equals the true cluster fraction, and clustering just those points recovers two modes. That single distribution describes the structure where every scalar failed — and it confirms §09: the two clusters are peers of each other, so a peer-comparison would have canceled them; you must calibrate against the breadth (the background).

12The proposal: the local concentration fieldsynthesis

Put the pieces together. The deliverable is not a scalar — it is a field and its distribution, with scalars read off it.

  1. Local primitive. For each item, the concentration of its \(k\)-NN neighborhood — the resultant length \(\bar r\) of its neighbors (§07). \(O(nkd)\), no clustering required.
  2. Breadth calibration. Standardize each item's concentration against what a same-size neighborhood drawn from the ambient breadth would give (§08–09) → a signed, two-sided per-item score.
  3. The field's distribution is the answer. Uniform space → one tight mode at the baseline. A global cone → one mode shifted high. A mixture → multiple modes (the §11 picture). Read off: fraction of mass above the breadth (how much of the space is crowded), number of high modes (cluster the flagged items), peak concentration (how bad the worst pocket is).
  4. Localize and explain. The high-concentration items, clustered, name the crowded sub-populations; an IsoScore\* per pocket (§10) says cone vs. offset ball.

Why this and not IsoScore: it is local (so non-collapsibility, §05, can't hide a pocket), calibrated against the breadth not peers (so §09's trap is avoided), two-sided (crowded cones and over-dispersed pockets), size-robust (§08), and it returns a structure, not a single confounded number. IsoScore and effective rank become supporting global context, not the verdict.

What we settled on. Unsupervised at the core — relevance (§13) is an add-on only when labels exist. The breadth reference is internal (the dataset's own field), with a synthetic isotropic cloud as a second yardstick for whole-space crowding. The deliverable is the distribution, not a scalar; and it is multiscale from the start, since pocket size is unknown. The next section walks the whole pipeline through in plain terms.

part v · what “resolution” really means

13Two kinds of crowdingrelevance · margin · floor

One loose end. Everything so far treats a tight, low-margin pocket as a problem. But the same geometry has two opposite causes — and no purely geometric measure can tell them apart.

Inside a cluster of genuinely related items, split each vector into the part they share and the part that makes them different:

\[ x = \underbrace{\alpha\,u}_{\text{shared}} + \underbrace{r}_{\text{residual}}, \qquad \lVert r\rVert \ll \alpha. \]
where\(x\) — an item's (unit) vector in the related cluster\(u\) — the shared direction the cluster has in common\(\alpha\) — how much of \(x\) is that shared component (large)\(r\) — the distinguishing residual: what makes this item different (small)

Cosine is dominated by the shared term \(\alpha u\), so the residual \(r\) — the very thing that distinguishes “128GB” from “256GB,” or “good” from “not good” — barely moves it. The pocket is tight and its within-pocket NN margins are near zero, exactly like pathological crowding. Yet these items should be close; the only failure is that the distinction is a sub-resolution residual.

cross-crowding
unrelated items, should be far apart. Impostors intrude; precision dies. The embedding is wrong.
fine-grained crowding
related items, should be close but rankable. The meaningful residual is below resolution. The embedding is coarsely right.

Unsupervised, the two are identical — high concentration, near-zero margins. The only thing that separates them is whether the near neighbors are actually relevant — the ground-truth signal the geometry can’t see. Cross-crowding pulls in non-relevant items (impostors are near): a precision failure. Fine-grained crowding stays relevant (the near-ties are all siblings) but unranked. Either way it takes a relevance label or a coarse clustering — the metric can’t supply it.

And under all of it is a hard floor. When the meaningful distinction maps to a cosine gap below the \(\sim 1/\sqrt d\) noise of §03 (plus finite precision and finite training), it is simply gone — no measure surfaces it and no retrieval trick recovers it. There the fix is not a better metric but a stronger, more discriminative embedding, or a re-ranking stage that re-reads the items.

What the measure should do. Flag the pocket, then characterize it with three numbers — concentration (how tight), NN-margin (how unresolved), and, with relevance labels, neighborhood precision (relevant near neighbors vs impostors). The first two are pure geometry; precision needs labels; and whether a high-relevance pocket is actually a problem is task-relative. That is where geometry stops and evaluation begins.

part vi · the solution, in plain terms

14The algorithm in plain Englishcone geometry · no formulas

Before the precise pipeline (§15), here is the whole idea in one geometric picture — no formulas. From §02, every item is a direction on a sphere, and two items are alike when their arrows point nearly the same way. So low resolution is a region where many arrows are crammed into a narrow cone: inside it everything points almost the same way, and cosine can no longer tell the items apart. That cone is what the method hunts for.

1 · Ask every point how tight its neighborhood cone is. Take an item's \(k\) nearest neighbors and average the cosine to them — that is the width of the cone that just holds them. The clean way to see it: add the neighbor arrows up. In a tight cone they reinforce into one long resultant; fanned out they cancel into a stub. That length is the tightness — one number per item, the field.

2 · At several zoom levels. We don't know how big a crowded patch is — ten items or ten thousand — so we ask with a small, a medium, and a large \(k\). A tiny knot only shows when you look at a few neighbors; a broad crowded swath only when you look at many. Each point keeps its most extreme answer.

3 · Against an even-cloud yardstick, because a raw closeness number means nothing alone. Build a perfectly even reference cloud — the same number of points sprinkled uniformly over the sphere — and measure its cones too. It is the no-structure null: in high dimensions even random points sit at a baseline closeness (§03), and the reference tells you what “spread out” actually looks like. Two reads come straight off it — a point is in a pocket when its cone is tighter than the reference's cones ever get (a calibrated cutoff, not an eyeballed gap), and the whole dataset is globally low-resolution when its typical cone is tighter than the reference's.

4 · Read the shape of the distribution — not one number. Histogram everyone's tightness. One bump near the reference = roomy everywhere; the whole bump shoved far above it = globally low-resolution (every neighborhood a cone — the dandelion); a separated dense bump = a localized pocket, its members the points that stand past anything the even reference produces (one bump each). A single average would melt a tight pocket into the roomy majority and hide it (§05) — the distribution cannot be fooled that way.

5 · Name the pockets. Group the crowded points that point in similar directions — one tight cone becomes one pocket, distinct cones stay apart — and describe each: how tight, how close its top-two neighbors are (can you even rank within it?), and whether it is a flat sliver or a round dense ball.

In one line. Every point asks “how tight is the cone of my nearest neighbors?” — at several sizes — and we compare that to a perfectly-even reference cloud (the null) — a point is a pocket when it is denser than the reference ever gets; the shape of the resulting distribution says whether low resolution is everywhere, nowhere, or pooled into pockets. The next section makes each step precise; in the report, RES 06 is that histogram and RES 07 paints it back onto the cloud. And a dense pocket is exactly where the grouping–resolution budget (§01) has been overspent — items pulled so close the space can no longer rank them.
15The local concentration field, end to endthe pipeline

Set the covariance and the eigenvalues aside for a moment. The whole method is one question, asked of every item, at a few zoom levels — and then a single picture: the histogram of the answers. Here it is, step by step, in the order the code runs.

1 · The question we ask each item. Take an item, find its \(k\) nearest neighbors by cosine, and average the cosine to them. That average is exactly the resultant length \(\bar r\) of §07 — how tightly the neighborhood points one way. Near \(0\): the neighbors are scattered over the sphere — roomy, plenty of room to rank them apart. Near \(1\): the neighbors are piled right on top of the item — crowded, nothing to separate them. One number per item; the whole array is the field. Because every item uses the same \(k\), all neighborhoods are the same size, so the random-walk size confound of §08 is gone by construction — the per-item numbers are directly comparable.

2 · Asked at a few zoom levels. We do not know how big a crowded region is — it could be twenty items or twenty thousand. So we ask the same question at several \(k\) (say \(10, 50, 200\)). A tight little knot stands out at small \(k\) and washes away at large \(k\); a broad crowded swath only shows once \(k\) is large enough to reach across it. Each item keeps its most extreme answer across scales, so a pocket of any size has a scale at which it is visible. This is the “multiscale from the start” choice — there is no single right \(k\) to guess.

3 · A density-matched reference is the null. A field value of \(0.3\) is only high or low relative to what is normal here, so we measure the very same field on a synthetic, perfectly even cloud of the same size, dimension and density — the isotropic reference, a no-structure null.

  • Detecting pockets. Per scale, standardize the field to a robust \(z\) (median / MAD) and flag an item as dense when its \(z\) passes the reference's own upper-tail threshold. The cutoff comes from the reference, not from the height of the dataset's own bulk, so a small but cleanly separated pocket is caught however tall the bulk. The contrast with a tempting alternative makes the choice concrete: a rule that flagged a separated mode only when a histogram bin cleared, say, 2% of the bulk's tallest bin would miss exactly the pockets that matter — a clean pocket of a few dozen items, spread across a few bins, can sit at a handful of items per bin while the bulk's peak bin holds hundreds, so its bins never clear a bar set as a fraction of that peak, however empty the valley separating it. Anchoring to the reference's tail instead is scale-free in the bulk's height: it asks only whether a value is extreme for an isotropic cloud of this size and density, which a real pocket's value is and a fraction-of-the-peak bar cannot see.
  • Global crowding. If the dataset's whole bulk sits far above the reference, the entire space is denser than uniform: the dandelion / union of cones, where every neighborhood is tight yet the cones point every which way. The reference is what catches this whole-space shift — a uniformly-dense field has no internal outliers, so it yields no pockets, only a shifted bulk (§09).

4 · The shape of the histogram is the answer. Plot the field and read the structure straight off it — no scalar in between.

Everything you want is read off this one distribution: the mass above the valley is how much of the space is crowded, the number of high bumps is how many pockets, the height of the worst bump is how bad it gets, and the gap to the isotropic reference is whole-space crowding. We deliberately do not crush it back into a single number — that is the move §05 showed is non-collapsible.

5 · Name and characterize each pocket. Cluster the reference-flagged dense points by angular proximity — an agglomerative cut tuned so one tight cone stays one pocket and distinct cones separate (never forced into a fixed number of blobs). The clustering doubles as the false-positive filter: the scattered, near-orthogonal points the tail threshold lets through do not form an angular cluster, while a real pocket does. Then describe each pocket with a few honest numbers: concentration (how dense), NN-margin = top-1 minus top-2 cosine (how unresolved, §13), and — as a secondary shape note, not the headline — IsoScore\* (a round dense ball vs a flat sliver, §10). When labels are present, add neighborhood relevance: are the near neighbors genuine siblings, or impostors (§13)? That overlay is the only place labels enter — the spine is fully unsupervised.

What you see in the report. Two coupled views, and no headline scalar in the facts row:

  • the field histogram itself — the artifact above, with the valley and the isotropic reference marked;
  • the existing 2-D / 3-D projection, recolored by each item's field, so the pockets light up exactly where they sit. The caption states the read in words.
Why this, and not a global IsoScore. It is local, so non-collapsibility (§05) cannot hide a pocket; calibrated against a density-matched reference, so the detection cutoff comes from the reference rather than the height of the dataset's own bulk; multiscale, so there is no pocket-size to guess; and it returns a structure, not one confounded number. Mean cosine, IsoScore and effective rank stay on as supporting global context — never the verdict.
What the field is — and what it is not. It is a k-NN density estimate in cosine units: monotone in local density, so it ranks neighborhoods reliably, but the magnitudes are not calibrated densities (the cosine→density map is strongly nonlinear in ~768-d). And density estimation in high dimensions is genuinely hard — distance concentration compresses the dynamic range, hubness biases who counts as a neighbor, and fixed-\(k\) under-reads the edge of a dense region. So read the field as a relative density map and a calibrated pocket detector, not as absolute densities. (A pocket's shape — a round dense ball vs a flat low-rank sliver — is a secondary annotation via IsoScore\*; density, not rank-collapse, is what this measures.)
in one breath Embeddings are directions on a sphere where “random” means “orthogonal”; crowding is a region where unrelated items get squeezed together; it is almost always local, and a global scalar is non-collapsible over clusters (a Simpson's paradox of isotropy), so it hides or misreads the pocket. Measure crowding as the length of each item's local average direction, calibrated against the breadth of the space — and report the distribution of that field: its modes, the mass in them, and where they are. That describes the anisotropy a single number cannot.
